## The LTV:CAC Ratio — Alex Hormozi
*Source: "Business Owners: You NEED to Know This Number," transcript*

**Best for:** Sizing up an offer before you write for it — whether the unit economics can afford aggressive acquisition, and how to stack front-end and back-end offers so the copy has room to work.

**Core thesis:** The single ratio that governs a business is lifetime gross profit divided by cost to acquire a customer. Nearly everyone miscalculates LTV by using revenue instead of gross profit, which hides the fact that they are turning $2,000 into zero. When the ratio is genuinely large, you can outbid every competitor for attention, absorb inefficiency, and scale into cold traffic, which is where wealth is actually made.

### Laws and principles

- **Compute LTV as lifetime gross *profit*, never revenue.** Price minus cost of goods or cost of delivery, times number of purchases or months retained. "Most people say lifetime value and they think okay well somebody pays me $1,000 a month for five months that means their lifetime value is $5,000 — no." Service businesses get this wrong far more often than physical-product businesses, because they treat payroll for delivery as if it weren't cost of goods.
- **Exclude fixed costs from gross profit.** Rent and non-delivery staff don't count. The point of gross profit is the marginal dollar you can reinvest in acquisition or pocket.
- **Two ways to get LTV, both valid:** price × months retained, or price ÷ churn rate. $2,000/mo at 20% monthly churn = $10,000 LTV = $2,000 × 5 months. If you're too new to know retention, run one cohort: of the 10 customers you started the month with, how many remain 30 days later?
- **CAC is the easy number.** Total ad spend + sales and marketing payroll + commissions over a period, divided by customers acquired. Use a full year if you can; it strips out campaign volatility.
- **Ratios in practice.** 5:1 can still lose money if LTV was computed on revenue. 7:1 is workable but slow. 30:1 is a real business. 100:1 is "a license to print money": you can become ten times *less* efficient at advertising and still run 10:1. Hormozi's first year of Gym Launch: "I spent $100,000 and made $10 million back."
- **A big ratio buys the right to a rising CAC.** Scale forces you into colder audiences and new channels, so CAC always climbs. "Let's say that Starbucks' cost per customer went from $10 to $50 — well, boohoo, still really really good."
- **Ad markets are auctions, so LTV is a legal monopoly.** "If you can outspend 100% of your competition, then you can ethically take all of their money and all of their customers." Between large competitors, CAC is roughly equal; the winner is whoever stacked more LTV.
- **Every activity should ladder to one of two levers.** "If what you're doing is not getting you more customers cheaper or making those customers worth more, then you are probably wasting your time."
- **The only exception to "sell the highest-ratio offer" is a tiny total market.** Most businesses are nowhere near saturation.
- **Operational efficiency is already priced into the ratio.** A lower-ticket, productized offer can beat a higher-ticket custom one because delivery cost collapses and cycle time shortens.

### Frameworks and formulas

**The core ratio.** `LTGP ÷ CAC`, where `LTGP = (Price − COGS) × purchases` or `(Price − COGS) ÷ churn%`.

**Front-end / back-end LTV stacking.** The hair-salon case: a $2,000 extensions course converting at ~$67 CAC (30:1) and a $15,000 business-coaching program at ~$2,000 CAC (7:1). The fix is not to advertise both. Stop advertising the expensive one, make the cheap high-ratio offer the sole front end, and ascend buyers into the back end, "which means your cost to acquire the customer here becomes zero." Blended LTV = $2,000 + (20% × $15,000) = $5,000, taking the ratio from 30:1 to 75:1. **Rule: advertise the offer with the best LTV:CAC, then stack everything else behind it.**

**The eight LTV levers,** applicable to any product:
1. Raise the price.
2. Decrease cost of goods or cost of delivery.
3. Get them to buy more times (increase frequency, decrease churn).
4. Cross-sell something different (burger → fries; phone case → wallet).
5. Upsell a **better** version (burger → sirloin → wagyu).
6. Sell **more** quantity.
7. **Downsell** a worse version, turning a no-sale into a sale.
8. Sell **fewer** units (three-pack → one-pack) as a downsell tier.
   Selection rule: pick whichever is easiest to implement now **without creating operational drag**. "Me writing a second book, probably a lot of work. Me asking people to buy a second book, much less work."

**The better/more/cheaper/fewer grid.** "Every product that exists in the marketplace, you can find a better version, a more version, a cheaper version, a fewer version, a cross-sold version." That's the full upsell and downsell architecture in one line.

**Build LTV back-to-front; build CAC front-to-back.** Design the most expensive back-end offer first and work backwards. But optimize the *funnel* from the front, because early steps affect the largest percentage of people: a 1% click-through can become 3%, but no show rate goes from 60% to 180% and no close rate goes from 35% to 105%. Exception: a clear outlier (a 5% show rate against a 60% benchmark) is a 12x sitting there.

**CAC is a percentage cascade.** Ad reaches 100% → click → schedule → show → buy → upsell. Lower CAC by either buying cheaper eyeballs or raising conversion after the click. "The closest thing to a silver bullet in the CAC world is nailing your offer."

**The shower-door test (productization).** Find the offer that (a) many people already want, (b) they understand instantly, (c) is cheap and fast to deliver, (d) has a big market. Shower doors over custom glass; modular pools over resort pools. Lower ticket, higher ratio, repeatable machine.

### How this translates to copy

**Ask before writing a word:**
1. What is the *gross profit* per customer, not the revenue? What's cost of goods, and does it rise with volume?
2. What's the current CAC and the ratio? At 5:1 or below, the copy is being asked to do a job the economics won't fund. At 30:1+, you have room for volume plays, free-plus-shipping, and cold traffic.
3. What's the churn or repeat rate? Is this a one-shot sale or a continuity asset?
4. Is there a back end? What percentage ascend, at what price? Write the front-end copy knowing the real target is the blended LTV.
5. Is this the *highest-ratio* offer in the catalog? If the client is advertising the expensive back-end offer directly, the copy fix may be a funnel fix.
6. What's the total addressable market? Saturation is the only reason not to scale a winner.

**How offer strength changes the approach.** A strong ratio means you can afford a low-friction, broad, front-end offer, because the money is made downstream. Copy can be simple, wide, and instantly comprehensible; it doesn't have to close a hard sale, it has to buy a cheap first step. A weak ratio forces the copy to carry everything: heavier proof, longer sales argument, higher-intent targeting, tighter qualification, and it will still be fragile. The higher-leverage move is usually to fix the offer stack before writing.

**Where copy has the most leverage.** Front of funnel: the advertisement, the offer, and the headline. "Most of the time the biggest gains are going to come from improvement in advertising, improving the offer, improving the headline."

**Write the downsell.** Every "no" is a zero. A junior version, a smaller quantity, or a stripped-down tier converts refusals into gross profit, and that additional LTV is what funds more aggressive acquisition copy.

### What this warns against

- Reporting revenue as LTV. The single most common and most expensive error.
- Service businesses treating delivery payroll as profit rather than cost of goods.
- Folding client ad spend or fulfillment into "revenue" and celebrating a 5:1 return while losing money monthly.
- Splitting ad budget across two offers instead of leading with the highest-ratio front end.
- Scaling past $1–3M without knowing these two numbers.
- Treating an offer or funnel decision as an opinion question. "This is a math problem."
- Chasing higher ticket for its own sake. Custom, high-ticket, hard-to-deliver work often has a *worse* ratio and a slower cycle than a productized, lower-ticket offer.
- Optimizing the back of the funnel first, where the percentage upside is capped.
- Adding LTV levers that create operational drag when a lighter lever is available.
