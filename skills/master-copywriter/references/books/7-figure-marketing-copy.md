## 7 Figure Marketing Copy — Sean Vosler

**Best for:** A framework encyclopedia for producing persuasive long-form copy fast — sales pages, emails, webinars, ads, content — by starting from a proven structure instead of a blank page.

**Core thesis:** Copywriting is a systems skill, not a talent. You don't write from scratch: you imitate proven structures, harness the built-in cognitive biases of readers, and run your message through repeatable outlines. The persuasive core of every piece is empathy plus an epiphany: acknowledge the reader's current belief, introduce a contrarian truth, prove it, and convert them from a buyer into a believer.

### Laws and principles

- **Write to a stated objective before you write a word.** Free-write to find it, throw the free-writing away, then state a SMART objective: "In this [email/page] I want to convince my reader that [feature] is better for [task] than their current method; I'll show [evidence], [case studies], and appeal to [emotion]."
- **Structure first, fill in the blanks second.** "The goal is to create a form for our copy FIRST than fill in the ______."
- **Imitate, don't copy.** Learning order: basics and terminology → imitation of style and form → spontaneous production. Study *how* trending headlines are structured, not what they say. Copying words verbatim is copyright infringement; copying structure is apprenticeship.
- **You are not the hero, your audience is.** You are the mentor or guide.
- **Sell insights, not solutions.** The best salespeople *challenge* the prospect's framing of the problem. They don't need a website, they need a lead-generation machine. Ask constantly: "Am I challenging my customer to think differently about their problem?"
- **Sell the benefit of the benefit.** They aren't buying hair product, they're buying an end to rejection. "What are you really selling? Sell that, not your solution."
- **Sell with emotion, justify with logic.** "Make 'em cry, make 'em buy."
- **Address the jugular objection first.** Identify the single most common objection and answer it in the opening. Cheat code: begin the intro with "Even if…" Illusory superiority means every reader's default reaction is "maybe for them, but not me."
- **Every claim needs at least one convincing piece of logic or evidence.** The bigger the claim, the more proof required.
- **The 2-to-1 benefit-to-risk rule.** Every purchase starts in a risk hole. Supply enough evidence that benefits more than double perceived risk, while amplifying the risk of *not* buying.
- **Loss looms twice as large as gain.** Emphasize what they lose by not acting before what they gain by acting. Reframe "Learn How to Write Better" as "How to Avoid Easy-to-Miss Writing Mistakes That Are Costing You Sales."
- **Cap choices.** Never more than three pricing tiers; strip lead forms to the minimum; remove the coupon field if you have no coupons. The confused mind says no. Use the **Rule of Two**: even with one offer, frame a choice ("Sign up" vs. "No, I'm not interested in learning about gardening") so the decision becomes conscious.
- **Impact + Frequency + Time = Winning Message.** Don't kill ads too early; the mere-exposure effect needs repetitions. Biases are an accelerant, not the engine. The message is the engine.
- **Anchor high before you price.** Compare to a full-time hire, a college course, competitors, or the cost of figuring it out alone.
- **Keep it simple, not simplistic.** Apply the grandma test. Revise the first draft solely to simplify and clarify.
- **Make attention grabbers relevant.** No one likes a tease; segue from the shock to the subject: "just like [grabber], you'll find that [subject] relates… here's how."
- **Forgive their faults.** "It's not your fault for not knowing." Forgiveness is the most robust trust builder there is, and it gives them permission to forgive themselves.
- **Define an enemy.** Three kinds: the **struggle** (what your features defeat), the **value battle** (Apple vs. PC, polarize on values), and the **boogie-man** (an enemy they didn't know existed). "A man with no enemies is a man with no character."
- **Raise local tension to universal tension.** Don't stop at the immediate problem (no sales); escalate to the big-picture stakes (a flawed approach to selling; a life of unfulfillment).
- **Repeat your core argument like a chorus.** Use anaphora, successive sentences starting with the same words, to give lists rhythm and memorability.
- **Bullets are your headline's best friend.** They summarize benefits, guide the eye down the page, reaffirm the headline's claim, and open loops.
- **Ego-morphing is real and forgivable.** People buy to confirm who they think they are. Polishing the ego-centric angle is ethical if you believe in the offer, offer refunds, and make no false promises.

### Frameworks and formulas

**Imitation Game (headline generation), 6 steps:** 1) Write a brief — what the reader should gain and be inspired to do. 2) Research trending headlines. 3) Formulate — strip the headline to a structure ("The Man Who Perfected The Laugh Track" → "The [noun] Who Perfected The [topic]"). 4) Develop — ask *why* it works. 5) Adapt — insert your audience and topic. 6) Expand — add a benefit modifier ("…and Learn How it 4x'd His Agency's Profits in 6 Months").

**Headline equation:** Attention-Grabbing Headline = "Audience Identifier" + "Hook" + "Benefit." Modifier types: End Result + Time Frame · Fear of Loss · Fear of Change · Fear of Unknown + Hope.

**Headline and sub-headline job spec:** Headline: 1) capture attention, 2) frame the problem. Sub-headline: 3) hint at solution, 4) address main objection. A headline must do at least one of: conjure curiosity, provoke thought, confirm suspicion, create intrigue, promise powerfully, question reality.

**Amazon R&D (subtitle mining):** 1) State the objective. 2) Go to Amazon's book section in your niche, sort by best sellers. 3) Map the *subtitle* structure and adapt ("A Counterintuitive Approach to Living a Good Life" → "A [adjective] Approach to [enticing promise]"). Subtitle archetypes: contrarian, intriguing, inspiring, powerful promise.

**Community Arbitrage (bullet generation from reviews):** 1) Find a best-selling book adjacent to your product. 2) Read reviews sorted by "most helpful." 3) Highlight lines that reveal inner dialogue and emotional pull. 4) Extract the essence into bullets and headlines.

**Bullet formula:** (Feature) + (Benefit) + (Benefit of that benefit). Rule: "If it's true, and it's a benefit to the reader, it's worth sharing."

**Full page skeleton:** Headline → Soft CTA/sub-headline (bridges headline to bullets) → Bullets → Hard CTA.

**The A's of Influence (15):** Attention · Appeal · Acknowledgement · Arousal · Angst · Aspirations · Attitude · Authority · Ambiguity (answer all objections) · Ascension · Affirm · Associate · Aggravate · Animate · Action. Master arc: *capture attention through appeal → influence attitude → arouse to action.*

**The Craft of Persuasion, 5 Foundations:** 1) **Fan Their Flames** — encourage the dream, close the gap. 2) **Forgive Their Faults** — it's not your fault. 3) **Defeat Their Demons** — kill the false beliefs with evidence. 4) **Prove Their Presumptions** — confirm what they already suspect. 5) **Attack The Enemy.**

**Forgiveness sub-formula (4 steps):** 1) Acknowledge a negative or false belief ("So you think you're bad at dating…"). 2) Give context for why they're not alone in holding it. 3) Forgive them: the misattribution of a situational issue to a value conclusion. 4) Invite them to leave the false belief behind.

**Mistakes Research Document (5 steps):** 1) DEFINE THE MISTAKES — three or more harmful beliefs. 2) RAISE THE STAKES — bullet the real-world consequences. 3) THE FORGIVENESS — list internal and external reasons they fell for it. 4) GRAND ILLUMINATION — immediate benefits, then extrapolate to higher-level benefits. 5) WHAT'S NEXT — concrete steps, one of which is your offer.

**Argument formula (myth repellant):** 1) Empathize with positive and negative views. 2) Present a claim. 3) Support with evidence. 4) Manage objections proactively. 5) Reach a beneficial conclusion. 6) Repeat.

**THE CONTRARIAN COPY STRUCTURE (the book's flagship):**
1. **ATTENTION** — Make a claim; hook to capture and hold attention; position the problem and why it matters; allegory or metaphor so they feel understood.
2. **ACKNOWLEDGEMENT** — What most people think (the misconception).
3. **AGGRAVATE** — Here's the truth on the matter (the claim).
4. **AUTHORITY** — Here's why it's true (the proof): scientific research, logical examples, emotional examples.
5. **ANGST** — The damage the false belief does: what it's done in the reader's life, where continued belief leads, the problem of the problem.
6. **AMBIGUITY** — What others or the author did to fix it: what to do now, the specific steps, beliefs changed, how to hold the change when it's hard.
7. **APPEAL** — Transition to the offer: summary of benefits plus call to action.
(Reported 9.4% cold-traffic conversion on the author's own page.)

**Contrarian Message Diagnostic (where the sale is lost):** If the reader thinks *it doesn't matter* → the stakes aren't high enough. If *it doesn't make logical sense* → not enough proof. If *I don't control this* → build a sub-argument against the underlying "I'm not that kind of person" belief. If *I have a gotcha* → find and address the outside counter-argument.

**Two conditions for real cognitive dissonance:** the reader must believe they have a choice, and there must be clear, meaningful consequences they care about.

**Hero's Journey research framework:** For each phase ask: their internal and external world; our role as guide; emotions to encourage or mitigate; tactical marketing assets for that phase. Depth of research scales with audience familiarity, price impact, and whether they're actively searching.

**Teach, Transform, Transact / Know–Like–Trust–Challenge–Convert:** Know (your story, why you sell it), Like (humor, personal stories), Trust (successes, video testimonials), Challenge (attack what they think they need), Convert (benefit of the benefit).

**Nostalgia story template:** A) name the audience, B) identify the shared belief, C) craft: 1) "A lot has changed, but, like you, we still believe…" 2) "…that ____ is still true/important." 3) "It may seem old fashioned to some, but I think you'd agree…" 4) "Remember when…?" 5) "Wouldn't it be great if…?" 6) "Well that's why we…"

**Staged FAQ:** list the risk-miscalculations (objections) → validate the concern → answer it → reframe toward the correct calculation.

**Seven framing tactics:** 1) Contextual Savings ("for less than a cup of coffee a day"). 2) Picking the Right Number ("200% growth" not "2x"; "80% fat-free" not "20% fat"). 3) Savings or Penalty. 4) Good vs. Evil. 5) Us vs. Everyone Else. 6) Unique in a Sea of the Same. 7) "Because I'm Worth It."

**Classic formulas reproduced in the appendix:** **AIDA** (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) and variants AIDCA/IDCA. **PAS** (Problem, Agitate, Solution), Kennedy's "most reliable sales formula ever invented"; variant Problem, Agitation, Discredit, Solution. **4 Ps** (Picture, Promise, Prove, Push) and (Problem, Promise, Proof, Proposal). **ACCA** (Awareness, Comprehension, Conviction, Action). **PAPA** (Problem, Advantages of solving, Proof, Action). **QUEST** (Qualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, Transition). **SLAP** (Stop, Look, Act, Purchase). **AIDPPC** (Attention, Interest, Description, Persuasion, Proof, Close). **AICPBSAWN** (Attention, Interest, Credibility, Proof, Benefits, Scarcity, Action, Warn, Now). **P.A.S.T.O.R** (Person/Problem/Pain, Amplify, Story/Solution/System, Testimonials, Offer, Response). **FAB** (Feature, Advantage, Benefit). **Jack Lacey's 5-Point:** What will you do for me? How? Who's responsible for the promises? Who have you done this for? What will it cost me? **6+1 (Danny Iny):** context, attention, desire, the gap, solution, call to act. **A FOREST checklist:** Alliteration, Facts, Opinions, Repetition, Examples, Statistics, Three (rule of).

**Elements of an Offer:** Here's what you're gonna get → Establish the value → Conditional bonus → Price → Trivialize price → Guarantee → Risk reversal → Scarcity.

**VSL script (Jim Edwards):** Shocking statement → state the problem and why it's a big deal → agitate → push agitation to a deep emotional level → introduce solution → credibility → proof → biggest things they get → reasons not to procrastinate → close asking for the purchase.

**Common VSL template (12 beats):** Attention-grabbing greeting · identify problem + promise to solve · scarcity · agitate · your solution as THE solution · features and benefits · CTA #1 (desire) · credentials, proof, testimonials · guarantee · CTA #2 (logic) · FOMO/deadline warning · CTA #3 (FOMO).

**12-Step Foolproof Sales Letter (David Frey):** 1 Get attention · 2 Identify the problem · 3 Provide the solution · 4 Present your credentials · 5 Show the benefits · 6 Give social proof · 7 Make your offer · 8 Inject scarcity · 9 Give a guarantee · 10 Call to action · 11 Give a warning · 12 Close with a reminder. Built to defeat **seven objections**: "You don't understand my problem" (empathy) · "How do I know you're qualified?" (authority) · "I don't believe you" (logical) · "I don't need it right now" (urgency) · "It won't work for me" (ego) · "What if I don't like it?" (risk) · "I can't afford it" (value). And it targets **seven universal motivations**: to be wealthy, good looking, healthy, popular; to have security, inner peace, free time.

**Know-Like-Trust-Challenge-Convert 5-email sequence:** #1 Know (who you are, why they should care, one teaching CTA, cliffhanger) · #2 Like (values, associations, cliffhanger) · #3 Trust (personal story of hardship → results, ask for engagement) · #4 Challenge (a customer story that proves the industry assumption wrong) · #5 Convert (the real solution, proof stack, offer, trial price, #1 reason to act now).

**Ad-analysis exercise:** 30 questions per swiped ad, including: Who is targeted? What's the short-term goal after the click? What's the price point? What's the core attention grabber? What dream does it inspire? What's the thesis? Does it take a contrarian POV? What enemy is defined? What objections are raised and how are they handled?

### Voice and craft notes

Conversational, self-deprecating, heavily aside-driven: parenthetical jokes, footnotes to itself, admissions of what the author doesn't know. Sentences are short and stacked; single-line paragraphs and one-word lines do heavy work. Anaphora and rhythmic lists are used deliberately, not decoratively. Metaphor and allegory carry the argument. Pop-culture scaffolding is constant, used as shared reference so the reader feels invited rather than lectured. Quotes open nearly every section, both to borrow authority and to set emotional key. Second person dominates; "you" is called out as the single most effective rhetorical device. The register is casual on purpose: "Casual is ok if your audience is casual." Colorful language, metaphor and simile are prescribed but balanced with substance, never flowery for its own sake, so "marketing that doesn't feel like marketing."

### What this book warns against

- Making the brand the hero instead of the customer.
- Fear-mongering and doom-preaching: "ethically questionable, and quite frankly lazy." Raise real stakes; dig for deeper, more universal risks.
- Overplaying emotion. Don't be a drama-king; intensity should match the magnitude of the topic. Subtlety beats overt.
- Flattery via the Barnum effect used to trick someone into buying.
- "Flash in the pan" copy: urgency, scarcity, authority and social proof used as brute force without a larger message. Once you've been found out, you lose trust for good.
- Hype-and-entitlement inspiration: "you deserve it," "just put your mind to it."
- Attention for attention's sake; hooks with no relevant segue.
- Too many choices, too many pricing tiers, too-long forms.
- Assuming your customer shares your motivations, or judging their reasons for buying.
- Turning ads off too early, before frequency has had time to work.
- Presenting features without context, jargon without translation, or diving into methodology just to sound smart.
- Copying swiped ads word-for-word, and starting research without a defined objective.
- Arguing with ideologues.
- Demanding too much *change* early. Presenting an entirely new journey is easier than asking someone to change.
- Writing copy for products you don't believe in.
