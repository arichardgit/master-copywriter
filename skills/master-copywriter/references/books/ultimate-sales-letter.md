## The Ultimate Sales Letter — Dan S. Kennedy

**Best for:** Building a long-form direct-response sales letter from scratch using a repeatable step-by-step process, when you need a letter that produces measurable orders.

**Core thesis:** A sales letter is a sales presentation in print, nothing more and nothing less. Persuasion in print obeys the same laws as persuasion on a living room floor selling a vacuum cleaner: understand the person, address their priorities not yours, agitate a problem, prove the claim, kill the objections, and force a decision now. Creativity is largely irrelevant; preparation, systematic assembly, and shameless recycling of proven structures do the work.

### Laws and principles

- **Enter the conversation already running in the prospect's head.** Answer the *10 Smart Market Diagnosis and Profiling Questions* first: (1) What keeps them awake at night, indigestion boiling up their esophagus, eyes open, staring at the ceiling? (2) What are they afraid of? (3) What are they angry about, and who at? (4) What are their top three daily frustrations? (5) What trends are occurring in their lives or businesses? (6) What do they secretly, ardently desire most? (7) Is there a built-in bias to how they decide? (8) Do they have their own language? (9) Who else is selling something similar, and how? (10) Who else has tried and failed, and how? You cannot persuade a person you cannot picture.
- **Walk a mile in their shoes, literally if necessary.** Drive through their ZIP codes. Read years of back issues of their trade journals. Eavesdrop in convention hotel bars. Take one to lunch and pump them. Play prospect with your competitors. Get to where you can visualize their day: how their mail arrives, who screens it, where they stand when they open it.
- **Address their priorities, not yours.** The failure mode of nearly all mail is talking about what the sender needs.
- **People do not buy things for what they are; they buy things for what they do.** Convert every feature to a benefit. Then hunt for the *hidden benefit* (Ted Nicholas), the one of profound private importance ("Puts Recruiting On Auto-Pilot So You Can Go Play Golf!").
- **The damaging admission.** Identify and openly name the flaws in your product, service, or offer. Admitting drawbacks makes your credibility stock go up. Turn competitive disadvantages into the selling story.
- **Figure out why they *won't* respond.** Write every objection, fear, doubt and excuse on a separate 3x5 card. The letter has to answer every one, because unlike a live salesperson it gets no feedback and cannot tell when enough is enough, so it must do more than enough.
- **Takeaway selling / intimidation.** The hardest deal to close is the one you desperately need. Take a position: limited quantity, application processes, qualification requirements, "only 5 out of 100 will respond."
- **America sorts its mail standing over a wastebasket** (Gary Halbert). Getting delivered and getting opened are separate engineering problems that must be solved *before* the copy is written.
- **Never be half pregnant.** Either sneak up (plain envelope, live stamp, no company name) or make the entire envelope a selling billboard. Nothing in between.
- **Always fulfill the envelope's promise inside.** Unfulfilled envelope promises destroy the credibility of everything enclosed.
- **Write for the buyer, not the nonbuyer.** Long copy works because real prospects are hungry for information. Never write to a predetermined length. Kennedy runs 4 to 16 pages and has repeatedly seen response climb as pages were added.
- **They buy on emotion and justify with logic.** Cold-fish, purely factual letters fail almost every time. What feels overly expressive when written still reads as understated.
- **The number one sin is being boring.** The problem is never length; the problem is dullness.
- **Believability beats credibility.** Testimonials about real experience outsell years-in-business statistics.
- **We accept new ideas only two ways: shock or repetition.** Since you can't shock most readers, use internal repetition. Tell them seven times, differently each time.
- **Good sales strategies don't wear out.** What worked in 1950 works in 2050 with slight language modification.
- **The Sophistication Trap.** Stubbornly believing your customers are smarter and more sophisticated than they are will cost you money. Study the ads in the *National Enquirer*, not the articles. Err toward simplicity.
- **Sequence beats the one-shot.** Mail three letters over 45–60 days, modeled on collection-agency first/second/third notice. Letters two and three combined often double the response of letter one.
- **It is easier to sell more to customers who know you, like you, and trust you.** Mail your own list a new offer every month.

### Frameworks and formulas

**The Kennedy System, 28 steps.** 1. Get "into" the customer. 2. Get "into" the offer (use the product; build features/benefits on 3x5 cards, sort by importance; find the hidden benefit). 3. Create a damaging admission. 4. Get the letter delivered. 5. Get it looked at. 6. Get it read. 7. Beat the price bugaboo. 8. Review winning techniques. 9. Write the first draft, no editing, no length worry. 10. Rewrite for strategy. 11. Rewrite for style. 12. Answer questions and objections. 13. Spark immediate action. 14. The creative P.S. 15. Check the checklists. 16. Use graphic enhancement. 17. Rewrite for passion, edit for clarity. 18. Compare your draft to examples. 19. Pretest. 20. Bring the letter to life. 21. Change graphic enhancements. 22. Edit again. 23. Mail a mockup to yourself. 24. The cool off, 3–5 days. 25. Get second opinions. 26. Final review. 27. Go to press. 28. Mail.

**Twelve headline formulas.** THEY DIDN'T THINK I COULD ___, BUT I DID · WHO ELSE WANTS ___ · HOW ___ MADE ME ___ · ARE YOU ___? · HOW I ___ · HOW TO ___ (optionally preceded by a flag: "For Busy Doctors:") · IF YOU ARE ___, YOU CAN ___ · SECRETS OF ___ · THOUSANDS/HUNDREDS/MILLIONS NOW ___ EVEN THOUGH THEY ___ · WARNING: ___ · GIVE ME ___ AND I'LL ___ · ___ WAYS TO ___.

**Three letter formulas that transcend price.** (1) **Problem–Agitation–Solution** — state the problem plainly enough to win agreement, then inject emotion: anger, resentment, guilt, embarrassment, fear. Make the problem larger than life; make them see the hearse backed up to the door. Only when they're pacing the room saying "this has got to stop!" do you unveil the solution. (2) **Fortune-telling** — predict a trend or coming crisis; be predictive and provocative. (3) **Winners and Losers** — the 5%/95% comparison, two neighbors, two graduates.

**Price minimizers.** Compare apples to oranges and do the arithmetic *for* them. Sell bulk ("1,001 pieces of information"). Discuss the price paid to develop the offer. Make the parts worth more than the whole: itemize each component with a dollar value until hard value dwarfs the fee, then add free bonuses. Conceal the price in installments: "3 payments of $19.95" outsold "$39.95" at a higher total.

**Five copywriting techniques.** #1 Intimidation/takeaway: limited number, "you will buy only if," "you can buy only if" (applications, affidavits, references). #2 Demonstrate ROI, sell money at a discount; exaggerate then walk it back with "even if I'm only half right." #3 Ego appeals. #4 Strong guarantee. #5 Be a storyteller; study fiction writers.

**Five guarantee types plus one advanced.** (1) Basic money-back, embellished with livelier wording than "satisfied": delighted, thrilled, "no hassles, no hard feelings." (2) Refund and keep the premium. (3) Redundancy: "a full 100% refund of every penny you paid." (4) Free trial offer framing. (5) Make the guarantee the primary focus/lead of the offer. Advanced: **guarantee the letter itself**, pay the reader $25–$50 if he feels his time was wasted. Costs Kennedy under $200 per 4,000 mailed and lifts readership and response.

**Seven immediate-action devices.** Limited availability; premiums (often worth a 50% response lift, preferable to discounts); deadlines (handwritten or rubber-stamped in red); multiple premiums (raised average order 30%); discounts for fast response or penalties for slow; sweepstakes and contests; ease of responding.

**The four factors that guarantee a winner:** they know and trust the company; they like the product; they believe the urgency-building story; they find the premium exciting and desirable.

**The P.S.** Every sales letter needs one. Many readers skip to the end first; their perversity is your opportunity. The P.S. summarizes the offer, adds an extra incentive, and functions as a high-impact *second headline*.

**Strategic rewrite devices.** Double readership path: a skim path of big bold headlines, subheads, captions and boxed paragraphs complete enough to sell the impulsive reader, laid over a full analytical path of facts and figures. Internal repetition through six vehicles: straightforward statement, example, story/slice-of-life, testimonials, quote from customer or expert, numbered summary. Yes-momentum via easy-agreement questions and question subheads. Never end a page with a completed sentence; break mid-phrase, and add teaser copy at the page foot (a teaser is a headline for the next page). Use the first paragraph as an extended headline: sell them on reading the letter before you sell the proposition.

**Objection-handling formula.** "Our marketing experts tell us only about X% of people receiving this will respond… that bothers me personally… so I held a special brainstorming session just to figure out why you might say no. We could think of only three possible reasons. Here they are:" Then list and demolish each, with a direct answer, a verifying testimonial or case history, and a restatement of the guarantee.

**Twelve graphic devices** to add voice inflection: bullets, numbering, underlining, boldfacing, varied type, simulated handwriting in margins and P.S., boxes, asterisk lines, yellow highlighter overprint, screens, photos with type wrapped, and lots of subheads. The copywriter, not the artist, decides these. Split tests prove the "ugly" jumbled illustrated letter beats the neat, pretty one.

**Pretesting, free.** Read it aloud and fix every tongue-twister. Read it to typical prospects and watch for the acid test: do they start asking how to buy? Have a young child read it aloud to you and change every word they stumble on.

### Voice and craft notes

Kennedy writes the way a closer talks: short declaratives, fragments, deliberate repetition, and an unembarrassed hard edge. "Frustrate the English teachers" is doctrine. Schoolbook grammar is irrelevant; use odd punctuation, non-sentences, one-word exclamations, and choppy rhythm to push the reader forward ("It's a fact. It's guaranteed. It's proven."). He models the register on an excited ten-year-old rushing through a story without a breath.

He is blunt about self-interest and about his own tactics, and that candor is itself the technique: the damaging admission applied to his own prose. Where a claim could strain belief, he pre-empts it ("even if I'm only half right"), sources it, or names the objection out loud before the reader can.

Proof is handled with volume and variety rather than one big assertion: testimonials, case histories with real dollar figures and full names, expert quotes, before/after, numbered summaries, and the guarantee restated near every answered objection. He treats intangibles as things to be made tangible.

He insists on personality. "Some letters have personality and others don't." Passion is engineered, not waited for: his exercise is to write to a lover you're persuading to run away to the Bahamas. Then edit aggressively, cutting every word that fails to advance, strengthen, or reinforce the sales story. "We have many imitators, but no one who matches…" becomes "Our many imitators can't match…"

### What this book warns against

- Writing about your own priorities and features instead of the reader's priorities and benefits.
- Being out of touch with the customer.
- Perfectionism and being intimidated by writing. You don't need a perfect letter; you need one that works.
- Writing to a predetermined length.
- Cold, purely factual, cold-fish logic-only copy, and above all being boring.
- Gimmicks in place of substance. Say something of genuine importance instead.
- Unfulfilled envelope promises.
- Half-pregnant envelopes and self-defeating address designations: OCCUPANT, SALES MANAGER, POSTAL PATRON.
- Hiding objections. Marketers who think they can hide the objectionable issues grossly underestimate customer skepticism.
- Overestimating your customers' sophistication and literacy.
- Outright humor. It rarely works and only an experienced pro can carry it off.
- One-shot mailings, and Madison Avenue's slow brand-recognition version of repetition.
- Neglecting your own house list.
- Ending a page on a completed sentence; omitting the P.S.; leaving graphic decisions to typesetters and artists.
- Trusting graphic artists, ad "pros," or web "techies" on marketing matters.
- Skipping the mockup, the cool-off, and the second opinions.
- Believing guarantees are outdated. Rising cynicism calls for bolder guarantees, not fewer.
