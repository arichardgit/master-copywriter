## Great Leads: The Six Easiest Ways to Start Any Sales Message — Michael Masterson & John Forde

**Best for:** Choosing and constructing the opening 100–600 words of any sales message by matching one of six lead archetypes to the prospect's awareness level.

**Core thesis:** About 80% of a promotion's emotional impact comes from the first 20% of the copy. Because buying decisions are made emotionally first and rationalized second, the lead's only job is to move the prospect emotionally and buy the right to the next sentence, not to argue. Which of six archetypal leads you use is determined almost entirely by one question: what does your customer already know?

### Laws and principles

- **Write the lead first and hardest.** It is 75–80% of the work. Read your own lead and ask: "Does this get me excited? Does it make me want to keep reading with high anticipation?" If not, rewrite it. The promotion will fail regardless of what follows.
- **Persuade emotionally before you persuade intellectually.** You have 100–600 words to incite an emotional response. There isn't time for a rational argument, and attempting one forfeits the heart.
- **Obey the Rule of One.** One big idea, one core emotion, one captivating story, one single desirable benefit, one inevitable response. Competing ideas dissipate emotional power; the "tossed salad" approach is B-level work.
- **Judge an idea by four tests:** big enough to stir interest, easy to understand, immediately convincing, clearly useful to the reader. Easy-to-believe is the hardest and most important of the four.
- **Ask "what does my customer already know?" before writing a word.** It decides lead type, headline words, opening line, even layout.
- **Plot the prospect on Schwartz's five-level awareness line:** Most Aware (knows product, only needs the deal) → Product-Aware (knows what you sell, unsure it's right for him) → Solution-Aware (knows the result he wants, not that you provide it) → Problem-Aware (senses a problem, doesn't know a solution exists) → Completely Unaware (knows nothing but his own identity or opinion).
- **Awareness sets directness.** The more aware, the more direct the lead. The less aware, the more indirect.
- **Go direct when:** the product is easy to understand; the promise is large and easily accepted; the deal or guarantee is exceptional; the customer knows and trusts you; you've made an improvement the market was waiting for.
- **Go indirect when:** the customer trusts you less than you imagine; doubts the scope of your claims; doesn't believe a solution is possible; doesn't know he has a problem; or your claims sound like everybody else's.
- **Beware the too-aware prospect.** Jaded, over-pitched buyers may score "most aware" yet need an indirect lead to rebuild trust. A simple Offer Lead to a burned buyer is suicide.
- **You cannot create desire, only awaken and channel what's already there.**
- **Specificity is required to overcome the skepticism that big claims and secrets automatically evoke.** Do the math for the reader. Attach dates. Name institutions.
- **Every lead, however indirect, must eventually return to a big claim, then the product, then the offer.**
- **The Golden Thread:** product at one end, prospect's heart at the other. Every element must connect to both, and the thread must stay taut. Slack for even a second loses the sale.
- **Test relentlessly.** Anything you can test in the closing offer, you can test in an Offer Lead.

### Frameworks and formulas

**Lead length and transition.** A lead is 100–600 words (300–400 is typical); its span is exactly as long as it takes to win emotional commitment to read on. The lead ends where the emotional hook is set, then you may switch to more direct tools inside the same package. Restating the headline claim in different words in the first lines of body copy is a common, successful pattern: it feels like progress and increases credibility.

**Rule of One, applied to a lead:** lead with one and only one powerful idea; make that idea create one emotion that compels response; support it with one engaging story or compelling fact; direct the reader to one and only one action.

**Caples' four elements every great lead conveys:** one strong idea, one desirable benefit, one driving emotion, one inevitable solution.

### THE SIX LEADS

**1. Offer Lead — Most Aware.** The most direct type. Product, price, discount, premium, guarantee appear in the headline or first lines. Formula: (1) immediately focus on the most emotionally compelling detail of the offer; (2) underscore the most valuable benefit of that deal; (3) elaborate on that deal-benefit through the lead; (4) include a compelling "reason why" you're offering it (short supply, deadline, news event) to answer "what's the catch?" Reveal or hint at any real catch early. Sub-type: the **Invitation Offer**, velvet-rope exclusivity, for clubs, societies, luxury, long-tradition products. Craft notes: endorsements and trusted signatures boost it; stay short; do the math; price cleverly ($1 trial, 2-for-1, trade-in); test time limits; make responding sound easy; "provide a parachute" by reversing risk in the headline ("Don't pay a penny until…"). Warning: heavy FREE premiums can cheapen a high-priced product and attract gift-seekers who cancel.

**2. Promise Lead — Product-Aware, "on the fence."** Slightly less direct; product mentioned later, but opens with the biggest, boldest claim. The promise is the headline, the first line, and often the last line. Build it from the USP (Rosser Reeves): the product must genuinely do it; it must be something competitors don't or can't claim; it must target a core desire the prospect already has. Rules: start with the product's biggest benefit; hit the targeted promise immediately; connect the benefit to the deeper emotional payoff (status, safety, inclusion, admiration), because how they'll feel and be seen matters more than what the product does; sound new and original; be bold but believable; follow with bigger proof; usually focus on speed, size, or quality of results. If a claim strains belief, either convert it to a question ("Can You Really Grow $500 Into $8.4 Million?") or scale it down until it's credible ("Get Rich Slow"). Fails with skeptics and unaware prospects.

**3. Problem-Solution Lead — Solution-Aware and Problem-Aware.** Delay the product; open by naming the prospect's biggest, most emotionally charged worry, then follow with promises. Formula: (1) target worries that keep customers up at night; (2) make sure they carry deep emotional weight; (3) stir those emotions first to prove you feel his pain; (4) don't linger on the problem too long before offering hope; (5) offer hope of a relevant solution at some point in the pitch. Find the worry by listening: customer service letters and calls, forums, blogs, conventions, for the core emotions beneath the surface problem (shame, guilt, inadequacy, loneliness, regret), what Makepeace calls "emotional relief benefits." Four working patterns: **If-Then** ("if you're feeling run down, then this will pick you up"); **Inversion / Negative Promise** (Schwartz's solution-first flip: "Get all the benefits of Summer sunshine but avoid the discomforts"); **Identification** ("For the Woman Who is Older Than She Looks"); **The Question** (an inevitable "yes" jump-starts the mental conversation); **Instruction** (coin a term such as "tired blood" or "worry stock" that instantly makes sense of the negative situation, then explain it).

**4. Big Secret Lead — Solution-Aware through Unaware; the dominant modern info-product lead.** The velvet pouch: describe the benefits of the thing while keeping it hidden. Four patterns: (1) the secret is intriguing **and** beneficial, tied to a major product benefit immediately; (2) it is introduced in the headline; (3) it is not disclosed anywhere in the lead, often never, until the prospect buys; (4) as the letter progresses, more clues are dropped like breadcrumbs that feel like revelation without revealing. Two ways to create the secret: find one already in the product (list every quality and component, pick the one nobody knows), or **neologize / transubstantiate**, renaming and repositioning an ordinary thing so it seems new and secret (dividend stocks → "the Chaffee Royalty Program"). Telling the reader what the secret is *not* makes him feel closer to what it is. Blends readily with Promise and Story Leads.

**5. Proclamation Lead — Completely Unaware (also works B2B to very aware pros).** Begins with an emotionally compelling statement, usually as the headline: a startling fact, a bold assertion, or a prediction, after which the copy supplies information demonstrating the validity of the implicit promise. Rules: (1) make it bold, not reasonable — startling, near-incredible, contrary; ordinary statements get skipped; (2) make or imply a promise, in headline or subhead, and make it concrete later; (3) the subject must be relevant to something the prospect cares about; (4) return to the proclamation at the close, in premiums and in the wording of the guarantee. Predictions gain credibility from a specific date. Proclamations "can't really be written, they have to be found": they come out of research, so collect amazing facts as you read. Prove them with anecdotes, charts, or metaphor.

**6. Story Lead — Completely Unaware; the most powerful and most indirect.** Well told, the reader forgets he's reading an ad and drops his advertising defenses. Construction: obey the rules of storytelling. Begin in the middle, with an expressed or implicit conflict, a protagonist the reader identifies with, and an emotionally satisfying resolution. The Caples deep structure to borrow: an ordinary person like your prospect attempts something extraordinary; people doubt him; he proves them wrong. A Story Lead always contains an embedded promise (learn what he did and you can do it too), and a secret is usually introduced inside the story or just after. Tell it quickly and keep it in the context of the core idea. Sources: testimonials, guru bios, historical proof, track records, your own experience. Give it "story appeal": a romantic situation, a memorable visual device. Critically, the product must be integral to the story (Ogilvy put Hathaway in the headline; the Dos Equis knockoff fails because the beer is detachable from the man).

### Voice and craft notes

Great leads read like conversation or reportage, never like advertising. Proclamation Leads read like newspaper stories. Story Leads read like fiction, in scene, with concrete visual detail. Sentences are short and cadenced; paragraphs are one to three lines; ellipses pull the eye down the page. The rhythm is claim → tiny specific → implied question → "let me show you why…" Each paragraph plants a question the next paragraph half-answers, which is how the next sentence gets earned.

Openers that work: a question the right reader must answer yes to; a flat startling assertion; a scene in mid-motion; a person named and dated; a direct offer of something free. Numbers are exact and odd ($20,727; 4,620%; 5.2 years; June 15, 2009) because roundness reads as invention. Objections are pre-empted in four sentences and dismissed. Third-party names carry credibility the writer can't claim himself. Repeat the headline claim in fresh words early; the reader reads it as confirmation, not redundancy.

### What this book warns against

- **Mismatching lead type to awareness.** A direct Offer Lead to an unaware audience bombs; an indirect lead to a most-aware buyer wastes the sale. Karen Reddel's supplement test: a straight Promise Lead beat a "muddy" Proclamation test by 50% because supplement buyers are highly aware. Conversely, Angela Salerno's Offer Lead did $1 million; recast as a Problem-Solution Lead for a broader, less-aware audience, the same copy did $3 million.
- **The tossed salad.** Dumping every feature and benefit in hopes one sticks.
- **Disclosing the secret in the lead,** the most common beginner error.
- **Claims so big they're unbelievable,** and Promise Leads that oversell a product into faster failure.
- **Being too subtle, too slow, or merely clever.** Indirect risks: choosing an indirect lead when you didn't need one; a hook too oblique to connect back to the product; boring the reader on the way to the offer; writing something interesting but irrelevant.
- **Pure news or curiosity with no connection to the customer's concerns.** When forced to choose, take the strong benefit.
- **A story detached from the product,** the Dos Equis error.
- **Believing static rules ("never more than seven words," "always give a premium") substitute for a big idea.**
- **Rushing to the "up" message** with a problem-aware prospect.
- **Naming the product in the headline of an indirect lead.**
