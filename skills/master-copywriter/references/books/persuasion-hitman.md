## Confessions of a Persuasion Hitman — Ian Stanley

**Best for:** Email copy, story-selling, and the economics behind the copy — average order value, offer stacking, and turning personal stories into sales assets.

**Core thesis:** Nobody has ever sold a product, a result, or a service. The only thing anyone has ever sold is a feeling. Persuasion is therefore not a bag of tactics but the by-product of empathy: find out where the prospect is right now, enter the conversation already in their head using their own words, take them on an emotional rollercoaster, and then make the math of buying obvious. The copy that makes the most money is usually the story you were most afraid to tell.

### Laws and principles

- **Set clear boundaries before you sell.** Define what you will and won't do for a sale, what you will and won't do after it, your expectations, and theirs.
- **Maximize Average Order Value on every purchase.** AOV determines how much you can pay for traffic, which determines whether you scale or die. A $7 ebook with a $40–44 AOV needed 375 sales/day to break even instead of 2,143.
- **Upsell only what is HIGHLY congruent.** Sell more of what they just bought, something that enhances it, something that gets the result faster, or a done-for-you version.
- **The first upsell is the most important slot.** Test your highest-priced product there. Judge it by dollars per buyer, not conversion rate.
- **Three ways to grow:** more front-end customers, higher AOV, or more and more-frequent purchases. "You have a PRODUCT, not a business" if you only have one thing to sell.
- **Get good at ONE skill.** He quit doing everything online, chose writing, and hand-copied a sales letter for an hour every single day without missing one. A month later he had a job writing emails to a 1M-person list at $20/hr; that same skill later billed $2,000/hr.
- **If your price doesn't make you slightly uncomfortable, it's too low.** Dan Kennedy: "Charge as high a price as you can while keeping a straight face." Raise prices at least yearly.
- **You don't get what you deserve; you get what you believe you deserve.**
- **Test price before design.** A $47 → $97 change held conversion rate constant and doubled revenue.
- **You're always selling a feeling.** The 8 core emotions: joy, fear, passion, love, anger, pain, shame, guilt. At every moment in a pitch you are either amplifying or reducing a feeling.
- **Create Feelings In Advance, not Results In Advance.** Let them taste the feeling before they buy.
- **Tell them WHAT, never HOW.** The what creates desire; the how is the product.
- **The more emotions felt in one sitting, the more they buy.** Don't ride one emotion. Make them laugh, then make them cry.
- **Attention is step one.** No attention, no pitch.
- **You don't matter until you know what matters to them.** Ask "Where are they NOW?" physically, mentally, emotionally, and in awareness level, before writing a word.
- **Acknowledge the conversation in their head, out loud.** Stanley opens comedy sets naming what the crowd is thinking about his face and accent; otherwise they think about it for the rest of the set instead of listening.
- **Do the math for them.** People are lazy and won't do it in their heads. Start with the highest number that logically fits and is believable; humans involuntarily anchor.
- **Use a price ladder: top rung first, then descend one rung at a time.** High price creates fear ("I can't afford this" → wanting it more); the cheaper option delivers relief, and relief is a buying state.
- **Combine short-term cash flow with long-term recurring revenue.** Break even on day 0–30 so you can scale.
- **The "Traffic Cliff":** scaling isn't linear. Going from $2.00 to $2.50 allowable CPA can win 2–5x more traffic, because competitors physically cannot follow you.
- **Sell in a way that doesn't create buyer's remorse.** Vanity sales don't pay; only fulfilled, non-refunded sales do. Watch profit, not revenue, views, or subscribers.
- **Don't have a "perfect" product.** The more flawless you appear, the less believable. Surface flaws and reframe them ("Proudly NOT Waterproof").
- **Handle objections in advance.** Per David Deutsch, this is what separates A-level copywriters from everyone else.
- **Know, Like, Trust.** Build the relationship before the pitch.
- **Use location changes to create "time distortion."** Moving a prospect between places, or shooting a video across office → warehouse → outside → kitchen, makes them feel they've known you longer, which raises trust.
- **Sell them what they want; give them what they need.** If your product genuinely helps, selling it is a moral obligation. This is not permission to bait and switch: sell the Ferrari, deliver the Ferrari.
- **Stop selling once the sale is made.**
- **Tell the stories that scare you most.** Connection happens around the rough edges; the energy in the writing transfers to the reader.
- **Templatize the close and the offer; stay creative in the open and the middle.**
- **Be different rather than better.** It's easier and it works.
- **Steal structures from OTHER markets, never your own.**

### Frameworks and formulas

**The 4-step face-to-face sale**
1. Get their attention, and qualify. Talk only to the unhappy.
2. Find out about THEM: current provider or solution, do you like it, what's missing, what drives you (price / quality / status).
3. Make a custom offer built from their answers.
4. Close by doing the math.

**Creating Feelings In Advance (the emotional close)**
1. Take them on the emotional rollercoaster: joy and laughter first to disarm, then the deep vulnerable story.
2. Aspiration: paint the after-picture with the word "Imagine." ("Imagine waking up six months from now. Your bills are paid…")
3. Make the offer.
4. Only if they haven't bought: the pain picture, the cost of doing nothing, same "imagine" structure, negative timeline.
5. Soften: "I'm not saying this to be harsh or cruel…"
6. Close with: **"If your life was exactly the same in six months, would you be ok with that?"**

**Pre-writing emotional audit**
1. What are they feeling now? 2. What do they want to feel? 3. What stories tap those feelings? 4. Build the bridge from now-feeling to wanted-feeling. 5. Hit as many of the 8 emotions as possible. 6. Build tension and desire, then let them release it by buying.

**Doing the math (close script)**
"So you pay $10,000 once and add $25,000 a month to your bottom line. Is that worth it for you?" Then escalate: "An extra $25,000 a month is $300,000 a year in found money. If someone said 'give me $10,000 today and I'll give you $300,000 at the end of the year,' what would you say?"

**First-line method**
- The purpose of line 1 is to get line 2 read; line 2 exists to get line 3 read (credit: Sugarman).
- Test: "Does this first line make it almost impossible for someone to leave the page?"
- Open a loop that can only be closed by reading on. Start outrageous, tone down later.
- Never open with your name or credentials. Removing "My name is Mike and I've been a Certified Nutrition Specialist…" produced a 22% lift; the replacement lead added another 20%.
- Training drill: go to a physical bookstore and copy down the first lines of thriller novels. Also collect first lines from old sales letters in other markets.
- When a line works, turn it into a template.

**Voice-of-customer research**
1. Get on the phone, with buyers and fence-sitters. Fence-sitter calls double as paid research.
2. Buyer questions: Why did you buy? What was the moment you decided? When did you decide I was the person to learn from? Was there a specific piece of content that sold you? What feeling did you want from this product? What feelings drove you to buy? What similar products have you bought and what did you like or dislike? What result do you want?
3. Reply-email version: *"Hey, just a quick question for you… What made you buy X product? I'd love to know so I can better serve you and our other customers. Just hit reply and let me know. Your answer can be one sentence like 'great price' or an entire novel if you feel like it."*
4. Mine Amazon reviews, subreddits, forums, Facebook comments, surveys.
5. Build a list of "customer phrases" and, one level deeper, "emotional trigger phrases."
6. Record sales calls, transcribe, add transitions. That's your sales letter (via John Carlton).

**Click Moment protocol**
Ask buyers the exact moment it clicked. Stanley's two: watching him write an email live on camera in 10–15 minutes, and "The Poseidon Story," a long email about his dog with no buy link. Once identified, do that thing every time and build campaigns around it. The dog story became his autoresponder opener and a 9-minute video: $187 spend, 2.4M views, 31k shares.

**Wants/Needs exercise:** two columns on paper. Everything they want; everything they need. Sell column one, deliver column two.

**Client-work rules:** won't start from scratch, only optimize funnels already selling; payment must be incentive-driven; ask "Do you care how much work I put in, or do you care that I win?"; set explicit boundaries; a real minimum up front.

2. "When I was 15 years old, 5 men stole my freedom."
3. "John didn't know he was gonna die that night."
4. "Joe didn't know his marriage was going to end that night."
5. "Time for a new twist on an old story you've probably already heard."
6. "Time for a story you probably won't read."
7. "Have you ever heard of your 'Personal Fat Threshold?' You probably haven't. It's not something your doctor will tell you about…because most doctors don't even know what it is…"
8. "What would it be worth to you to double your sales on just ONE of your funnels?"
9. (Customer's own words, used as line one of a skincare letter): "I just want to look in the mirror and feel pretty again."

Closes and transitions:
10. "If your life was exactly the same in six months, would you be ok with that?"
11. "I'm not saying this to be harsh or cruel. I'm saying it because I know that there is a cost to doing nothing. I don't want you to be in exact same place six months from now. I want a better life for you and I know you want that too. Otherwise you wouldn't have read this far."
12. "Now, I think you'd agree that $3.5 million in sales is worth paying me $100,000. But that's not what they paid. They paid me $50,000. $50,000 to make $7.5 million. Pretty good deal right?"
13. "Now it was time for the curveball."
14. "Ok, that's a little extreme…but I think you get the point."
15. "Boom. Free lemons."
16. "Which leads (damn near perfectly) to the overall rule of this chapter…"
17. Objection-as-feature: "Proudly NOT Waterproof. Your feet need to breathe, especially while moving. Waterproof boots act as a microwave for your feet…"
18. In-person upsell: "How 'bout a nice glass of lemonade to quench that thirst sir?" then "you're going to want to balance out that acidity with something nice and sweet."
19. Identity reframe: "I do NOT have Crohn's Disease. Crohn's Disease has Ian Stanley Disease."
20. "I've stepped over a lot of dollar bills to pick up pennies in my career. But now those pennies have turned into 10, 20, and 100 dollar bills."
21. "Connection happens around the rough edges."
22. Winning fruit-stand sign: "Picked Today."

### Voice and craft notes

- **Paragraphs are one to three sentences, often one.** White space is the default. Long blocks appear only inside a story's emotional peak.
- **Open cold, mid-action, with no throat-clearing.** No name, no credentials, no "in this email I'll show you." Compare: "When I was 22 years old I took a job selling DirecTV." "I sat on the couch with my buddy as I was sending off a proposal and asked if I should charge $20k or $25k upfront."
- **Fragments and rhythm breaks carry the punch:** "That's $50 per hour at 12 years old. Not. Too. Shabby." / "What. A. Waste." / "Boom."
- **The pivot from story to lesson is a single naked line, usually beginning "See," or "Here's":** "See, most sales people focus on creating one core emotion…" / "Here's where people make the mistake." The story delivers the emotion; the pivot cashes it in.
- **He telegraphs the turn before he takes it:** "Now it was time for the curveball," "Which leads me to…," "Back to Best Buy…" so the reader never gets lost.
- **Curiosity gaps are held open across pages.** He plants "the word 'install' is important, I'll explain in a minute" and doesn't pay it off for a full chapter.
- **Caps for emphasis, not bold:** THEM, NOT, FEEL. One or two words per paragraph, never a whole sentence.
- **Self-deprecation buys permission.** He mocks his own goatee, his cheesy phrases, his laziness. Then he claims expertise. The order matters.
- **Concrete numbers everywhere:** $63 and some change, 16–20 minutes a racket, 22% lift, 6,000 subscribers. Specificity is his proof mechanism.
- **He addresses the reader's live objection in parentheses:** "(I'm not saying these are typical results.)" The aside carries the joke and the disclaimer at once.
- **Analogy from a taboo domain, then immediate translation.** He builds desire theory on seduction and then converts it in one line: "It's the same in selling. It's about the PROSPECT."
- **Sensory second-person for the aspiration block.** Present tense, mundane detail, one image per sentence: "You look in the mirror and you're proud of what you see. You've lost 13 pounds and your jeans don't even fit anymore."
- **Escalation-to-absurdity as a comedy release valve,** then a wink and a step back.
- **Odd from-names as an open-rate device:** "Your Abs," "Your Wallet."

### What this book warns against

- Opening with your name, company, or credentials. Nobody cares yet.
- "We" copy: leading with who you are before what matters to them.
- Flaccid, lazy first lines.
- Copying what's already working *in your own market*.
- Trying to appear perfect, or hiding a product flaw instead of reframing it.
- Meeting every objection with a logical counterpoint that only pushes the product harder.
- Riding one emotion, usually fear, through an entire message.
- Continuing to sell after the sale is closed.
- Assuming people need your product, or that a great product sells itself: "No one gives a damn about your product."
- Bait-and-switch dressed up as "sell them what they want."
- Selling anything you don't believe in. Trust takes months to build and one second to lose.
- Chasing vanity metrics: revenue, views, subscribers.
- Doing everything yourself instead of one skill.
- Trading time for money in a skill that requires your presence.
- Never testing price because the software makes it annoying.
- Front-end offers built without congruent upsells planned from the start.
- Research as procrastination. Sometimes you know enough and need to write.
- Faking your prospect's lingo when you don't understand it. One wrong term can lose the sale.
