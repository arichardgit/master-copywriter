## High Probability Selling — Jacques Werth and Nicholas E. Ruben

**Best for:** The qualification, anti-hype, and trust authority. The book to reach for when the problem is that your copy is trying too hard to convince the wrong reader.

**Core thesis:** The objective of selling is not to get the prospect to buy. It is to determine whether there is a mutually acceptable basis for doing business. Persuasion causes resistance: every attempt to convince, pressure, or mask the selling objective generates more of it. So you stop transforming Low Probability Prospects and instead identify, as fast and cheaply as possible, the small group who already need, want, and can afford what you sell, are ready to buy now, and are willing to buy from you, then trade explicit commitments with them.

### Laws and principles

- **Sell only to people who already want it.** Prospects sort into four groups: (1) need, want, can afford; (2) need and afford but don't want; (3) need and want but can't afford; (4) all three but won't buy from you. Spend everything on group one. "The number of High Probability Prospects is usually small. Part of our job is to identify them."
- **Disqualify, don't qualify.** You do the dis-qualifying; the prospect does the qualifying with his answers. Give the prospect every opportunity to disqualify himself, early and often, even after he's already qualified. "A High Probability Prospect will seldom allow himself to be disqualified without a struggle."
- **Never ask for the order.** Negotiate mutual commitments instead.
- **Traditional selling gives the choice of Yes or Yes. High Probability Selling gives the choice of Yes or No.** That single difference is what removes resistance.
- **The takeaway is structural, not tactical.** "It's okay if you don't want to talk to me. Just say so, and I won't call again." Inviting "No" makes it easier for the prospect to say yes, because it hands him responsibility for continuing.
- **Refuse to convince.** "You don't ever try to convince a prospect that he needs and wants what you have to sell. If he clearly understands the benefits you're offering, and they're not what he wants, it's time to end that visit. If he really wants what you have, he won't let you go."
- **Interest is worthless.** "There is no sales value whatsoever when a prospect says he's 'interested.' There's no commitment in interest. It's usually a cover for someone who doesn't want to buy."
- **Pressure destroys conversion.** Whenever the seller is talking, the prospect feels pressured, and pressure generates objections.
- **If you feel manipulative, they feel it too.** Stress on a prospecting call, or a call running past three or four minutes, means you've slipped into selling.
- **Do business only with people you trust and respect,** and say so out loud. "People don't respect, or want to do business with people who don't have standards."
- **Trustworthiness cannot be performed.** "It's not what you're doing, it's who you're being." When people sense you are not attached to a particular result, they start to trust you.
- **Never guarantee unconditional satisfaction.** Some people can never be satisfied. Limit demands by listing the prospect's specific Conditions of Satisfaction instead.
- **Raise the objection yourself, early.** What traditional selling calls objections are just points to be addressed and negotiated. Surfacing a deal-breaker before you do the work is cheap; discovering it after is expensive.
- **Test the commitment after you get it.** "Are you sure that's what you want to do?" and "Why?" Weak commitments surface while you can still handle them; strong ones get restated in the prospect's own reasons and get stronger. This is why buyer's remorse and cancellations nearly vanish.
- **Don't respond to non sequiturs.** "I'm not interested" answers no question. Only a clear statement of no need, no want, or no money disqualifies.
- **Take responsibility for results.** "Don't blame the prospect when something goes wrong with the sale." And don't get angry at a Low Probability Prospect: "that's like getting angry because he's short."
- **Beware Random Negative Reinforcement:** occasional sales made while doing everything wrong reinforce methods that lose money over time, the slot-machine effect.
- **The primary price of success is discomfort.** If a new approach feels comfortable, you're doing the old one.

### Frameworks and formulas

**The three phases:** High Probability Prospecting → High Probability Selling (Establishing a Relationship, then Discovery/Dis-Qualification) → High Probability Closing (Conditions of Satisfaction). End the meeting if you cannot complete any phase.

**Languaging your offer (the prospecting call):**
1. State your offer in 45 words or fewer, including salutation and introduction, a fast concrete word picture. Shorter offers work better. Neutral, unemotional tone; a convincing tone itself causes resistance.
2. Ask if he wants what you're offering.
3. If yes, ask if he wants to arrange a meeting with the intention of doing business.
4. If not now, reschedule to a time he names, with his commitment to move forward then.
5. End the call on any negative answer. Assure him it's okay to say "No" whenever you sense hesitation.
6. Dis-qualify everyone who doesn't want it or won't buy from you. Never "sell the appointment."

**Pre-appointment dis-qualification checklist:** Does he want the product? Does he want an appointment? If not now, when? Does he want you to check back? Why does he want to meet now? What does he want to accomplish at the meeting?

**Establishing a Relationship:** Don't mention your product. Make an observation about the building, company, or trip and attach a question. Then ask how he got where he is. Ask open-ended questions — Why? What did you do? How did you handle that? Was that something you wanted to do? — each built on the previous answer. The purpose is to decide whether you trust and respect him, not to find hot buttons. It's complete when the answers turn genuinely personal.

**The 14 Discovery / Dis-Qualification questions** (asked about "this product," never "our product"; answers recorded in his exact words):
1. Why do you need this product? 2. Do you want this product? Why? 3. This product will cost approximately X dollars. Are you prepared to spend that? 4. If you decided to go forward, when would you want this to start? What if it doesn't start then? 5. When you're making a decision like this, who do you usually like to talk it over with? 6. If you decided to go forward, who else would have to agree? 7. What would happen if you don't purchase this product? 8. Suppose you had to decide right now, without talking to me or anyone else, which brand would you buy? 9. Is there someone you would rather do business with? 10. What's your procedure for issuing a purchase order? 11. What would it mean to you personally if you didn't purchase this product? 12. Is there any reason why you wouldn't want to do business with me? (pause) Something we haven't covered yet? (pause) An emotional reason? (pause) Anything? 13. What could be lurking in the background that would prevent this from happening? 14. **If I can meet all your criteria, what will you do?**

**High Probability Closing / Conditions of Satisfaction:**
1. Get the commitment first. Anything short of "I'll give you my business" means you set ground rules or disqualify.
2. Read back everything from Discovery; ask what else.
3. Record every condition in his exact words; raise conditions he hasn't mentioned. Cover price, service, quality. Price first, not last.
4. Make each condition measurable ("How will you measure that?").
5. Negotiate what you can't meet; state the negatives plainly; disqualify if it can't be resolved.
6. Confirm: "Has anything changed?" → "Are you sure?" → "Why?"
7. Only then demonstrate, quote, or propose. Nothing is built before commitment.

### Swipe: verbatim lines

- "Hello, Mr. Prospect, this is Sue Green from WPC Packaging. We manufacture self-contained, four-color display packaging, which is shipped flat and is easy to assemble. Is that something you want for your product line?"
- "This is John Boardman from the Protection insurance agency. I sell long-term care insurance, which covers the cost of nursing home care, or comparable at home care, not covered by Medicare. Is that something you want?"
- "Mr. Garson, it's okay if you don't want to talk to me. Just say so, and I won't call again."
- "Does that mean that you want what I'm offering, or not?"
- "Assuming we can work those things out, is this something you want, or not?"
- "Usually when people say that, it's a polite way of saying 'Get lost'. If you want me to 'Get lost', just say so. It's okay."
- "You don't sound like a High Probability Prospect. Is my product something you want, or not?"
- "I don't have a satisfactory answer to your question. What answer would make sense to you?"
- "If our packaging meets your criteria, what will you do?"
- "That's not the way I work. I'm not willing to prepare a proposal unless I have a commitment that we'll do business if I meet your criteria. What do you want to do?"
- "You don't have to make a commitment unless you want to. But I won't prepare a proposal for you without one. So, what do you want to do?"
- "If you're not serious about buying if we fulfill your Conditions of Satisfaction, just say so. It's all right to say 'No,' if that's what you mean. Just say so now, not after I've done all the work."
- "No, we don't print in metallic ink on the outside corrugated packaging. Metallic inks aren't durable enough to withstand handling. Will you accept a top quality four-color process, or must you have metallic printing?"
- "The way I do business is to learn as much as possible about my customers first... I also need to find out whether I trust and respect them. I only do business with people I trust and respect... So, are you willing to continue with this kind of conversation, or should I go?"
- "Are you sure that's what you want to do?" / "Why?" / "Is there any doubt in your mind...?"
- "Tom, you don't have to do anything you don't want to do."
- "If they can guarantee that quality at no additional cost, perhaps you should give them your business."
- "You seem upset." → "You still seem upset." → "Maybe we should discuss this at another time."

### How this translates to copy

Written copy is a prospecting call at scale: its job is identification, not conversion of the unwilling. Open by languaging the offer, a short, concrete, unhyped description of exactly what this is, and ask the reader directly whether it's what he wants. Lead with the qualification, not the sizzle: if you need a stunt to get attention, you don't have a High Probability reader.

Build the takeaway into the structure. Say who this is not for, and mean it. Name the disqualifying conditions ("if you want the lowest price, buy elsewhere"), state the negatives before the reader finds them, and give explicit permission to leave. This is the "or not" clause in written form, and it works for the same reason: giving the reader a real No makes his Yes his own, which raises conversion and collapses refunds, chargebacks, and buyer's remorse.

Replace persuasion with inquiry. Frame claims as questions the reader answers privately ("Are you willing to pay more for X?"), and let his own answers escalate commitment step by step. Never ask for the order in the begging register; ask for a commitment: *if this meets your criteria, what will you do?* Order pages, application forms, qualification quizzes, and price-first copy are all Conditions of Satisfaction made textual. Spell out precisely what the buyer gets, in measurable terms, so nothing is ambiguous enough to renege on.

Match your offer to a niche defined by your real combination of price, quality, and service. Claiming best-in-all-three is the lie every prospect discounts. Honest disqualification is the most efficient trust device in copy: a seller visibly willing to lose the sale is the only one the reader has reason to believe.

### What this book warns against

Selling the appointment; concealing what you sell; unsolicited information dumps; manipulating through Attention–Interest–Desire–Conviction–Action as a *selling* model; rhetorical and minor-point closes ("Do you want the blue or the grey?"); assumptive closes; hyped statistics used to entice ("Would you like to increase your sales by thirty percent?"); handling objections instead of surfacing them; unconditional satisfaction guarantees; aggressiveness and persistence; fawning, flattery, feigned interest, and manufactured rapport; talking more than 25 percent of the time; demoing, quoting, or building anything before a commitment; accepting evasive answers or non sequiturs; relationship building with prospects who have no urgency; responding to anger with emotion; blaming the prospect; and mistaking occasional lucky sales for a working method.
