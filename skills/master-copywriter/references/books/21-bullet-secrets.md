## 21 Bullet Secrets That Ignite The Selling Power Of Your Copy — Clayton Makepeace & Anthony Flores

**Best for:** Writing bullets and fascinations, the curiosity-driven benefit lines that carry book, newsletter, supplement, and info-product promos, and mining them for headlines.

**Core thesis:** Bullets are the highest-leverage micro-skill in direct response: benefit-packed gems that stimulate curiosity, seduce continued reading, and build desire line by line. The best writers don't improvise them. They research exhaustively, write far more than they need against a fixed library of proven formulas, then keep only the cream. Every bullet must carry four elements — benefit, intrigue, specificity, and credibility — and the mechanism behind the promise is what makes a claim believable in a saturated market.

### The 21 bullet types

**1. The "How to" bullet** — The most popular formula; taps our natural appetite for how-to information, strongest when tied to deep feelings and desires.
> "How to rub your stomach away. The simplest and most natural way to lose weight is by this effortless two-minute exercise."
> "How to make yourself safer than 89% of other car passengers — page 389."

**2. The "Secret to" bullet** — For genuinely uncommon information; the secret usually centers on *how* the prospect gets the benefit. Don't overuse or skepticism spikes.
> "Little known secrets that can steer you to tremendous profits in gold … even if bullion drops to $250 an ounce!"
> "The secret of 'that schoolgirl complexion' revealed! This is the natural compound you must have for health, beauty and long life."

**3. The "Why" bullet** — Promises to reveal *why* something is so, implying that knowing why changes the prospect's life. Ideal for intrigue.
> "Why you can't trust your pension fund: Dirty tricks they play with your money — and how to stop them COLD …"
> "Why up to 70 percent of all poisonings happen to children under five. And the best prevention of them all. Page 34."

**4. The "What" bullet** — Two modes: specific instructions for action ("What you MUST do now to avoid…") or elusive, withheld information ("What doctors don't tell you about…").
> "What you must do at once if you receive a mild shock when you touch an electric appliance — page 26."
> "What the Corporate Fat Cats don't tell you about their CoQ10 supplements …"

**5. The "What NEVER" bullet** — Alerts to a possible mistake (fear), promises protection (benefit), withholds the answer (curiosity). Variants: "Why you should not…", "The absolute worst…", "What to avoid…"
> "What never to eat on an airplane. The dirtiest, deadliest airplane in the whole wide world."
> "The One Place NEVER to Store Your Gold: It's practically an engraved invitation to thieves."
> "The absolute worst time for your body to do paperwork or read. Page 26."

**6. The "PLUS" bullet** — A greed bullet: any other formula prefixed with PLUS, putting the prospect in a more-more-MORE mindset. Best at the end of a list.
> "PLUS my complete list of 1,837 companies that we suspect of fudging their earnings — make sure your stocks are NOT on this list!"

**7. The "Number" bullet** — Groups multiple ways, secrets, or reasons into a value-added team of enticing items.
> "Four ways to stimulate the body to release its own natural painkillers."
> "The seven little-known signs of internal bleeding and what to do about each — page 4."

**8. The "Right … WRONG!" bullet** — States an assumption the prospect holds, then detonates it.
> "Sneezing into a tissue prevents colds, right? Wrong! Page 2 explains why."
> "The bathroom medicine cabinet is the best place to store medicine, right? Wrong! It's the worst. The facts are on page 120."

**9. The "WARNING" bullet** — Alerts to danger ahead; also CAUTION, ALERT, DANGER. Works only when you can prove you hold the solution.
> "WARNING: Avoid These 2 Popular Gold Investments Like The Plague! Ignoring this single warning could leave you broke and holding the bag!"
> "Cell phone DANGER! Avoid sleep disruption from electromagnetic waves …"

**10. The "Are you …?" bullet** — Asks about something he's almost certainly already doing, getting a yes-nod and momentum. Variants: "Do you…?", "Does your…?", "Is your…?"
> "Does your broker have a 'rap sheet' with the SEC or NASD? Here's how to find out — FAST!"
> "Do you take any of these 16 popular vitamins or supplements? If so, please read this M.D.'s urgent warning!"

**11. The "Gimmick" bullet** — Extract an idea from research, put a creative spin on it, and *name* it. Combines benefit, specificity, intrigue, and a precise mechanism in two lines.
> "The simple 'pinch test' that instantly tells what your body fat levels are — with pinpoint, .5% accuracy."
> "The 'optical illusion' that absolutely destroys most golfers … the single most damaging 'habit' nearly all amateurs suffer that GUARANTEES they will never experience a consistently great game!"

**12. The "Sneaky" bullet** — For information with a conspiracy factor, or something hidden, guarded, or overlooked. Used *very* selectively.
> "Sneaky little arthritis secrets that doctors never, ever tell you about …"
> "Sneaky insurance tricks that could be costing you an arm and a leg. Make sure you're not a victim."

**13. The "Statement of Interest + Benefit" bullet** — Lead with an arresting fact or scenario, then hit the benefit.
> "Drowning is the third leading cause of accidental death. But did you know it's possible to save a drowning person even if you can't swim? Page 15."
> "You walk into an empty self-service elevator late at night and press the button. Before the door has a chance to close you get back out fast. Why? (Nine ways to outwit a mugger.) Page 380."

**14. The "Direct Benefit" bullet** — Open with a bald claim, first word usually an action verb; the next line deepens, proves, and adds intrigue.
> "BUILD ENORMOUS STRENGTH AND MUSCLE TONE without exercise! Ten minute breakthrough naturally triples your growth hormone levels. Secretly being used by the New York Mets, Chicago Cubs and many Hollywood stars. All you do is stand."
> "BANISH MENSTRUAL CRAMPS FOR GOOD with a common vitamin! Now shown to wipe out over 80% of all cases of cramps."

**15. The "Specific Question" bullet** — Hooks with an intriguing question of importance; the answer lives in the copy or product.
> "Do you know that one-quarter of all household burglars gain entrance without breaking in? Here's how they do it … and how to get them to stop doing it to you. Page 157."
> "What time of day are you at greatest risk for low blood glucose? This surprising answer will put you on alert."

**16. The "If … Then" bullet** — Invites the prospect to meet a simple requirement or identify with a group, then pays off with the benefit. A Gary Bencivenga favorite for headlines.
> "If you have a tension headache, here's why you should forget your scalp and concentrate on your toes."
> "If you've got just 20 minutes a month, I guarantee to work a financial miracle in your life."

**17. The "When" bullet** — Promises a benefit at a specific moment.
> "When the IRS has to pay you interest! If they miss this deadline by a single day, they're legally obligated to do so. Page 15."
> "When cold pizza is the perfect fat-loss breakfast. Page 14."

**18. The "Quickest, Easiest" bullet** — Offers the fastest, simplest, least-effort route.
> "And finally, the easiest way to be a master of persuasion — the simple, 12 word sentence that will make you one of the most persuasive people on the planet, no matter what field you're in."

**19. The "Truth" bullet** — Settles a controversial or confusing issue. Variant: "The real cause of…"
> "THE TRUTH ABOUT TUNA. Is it good for you? Or does it contain harmful metals that can ruin your immune system? See page 70."
> "The truth about mutual funds. What your broker doesn't tell you could cost you up to 5% this year."

**20. The "Better" bullet** — When something is assumed good and you have better; slips under the BS detector. Variant: lead with "beyond."
> "BETTER THAN SQUATS! Try this leg exercise on page 29. It takes less time than squats and will not injure your back!"
> "BEYOND THE BEDROOM! Other rooms can spice up your sex life."

**21. The "Single" (Single Most) bullet** — For the one item that exceeds all others, and only when you have proof.
> "The single most important sentence you will ever read about how to create powerful marketing. It contains just nine words, and they will forever change your approach to marketing."
> "The single most explosive investment in this coming crash. It takes advantage of my three proven growth factors and is still available dirt-cheap."

### Laws and principles

**Your #1 job is to keep the prospect reading.** Long copy sells because reading it builds want, but only if he reaches the end. Bullets, being easy to read, are the deadliest potential weak links.

**Write vastly more than you need.** Parris Lampropoulos writes as many as 700 fascinations and keeps about 100, roughly seven times what he could ever use, so every survivor is cream. Gene Schwartz read the source book repeatedly, highlighted, had 60–70 pages of notes typed, then wrote fascinations while filling in running text.

**Attack every fact from 2-3 angles.** For each piece of information, two or three formulas will fit. Write a version with each, then move on. Combining formulas is the advanced move (Carlton's "10 'Come Hither' Sexual Signals" = Number + Gimmick + Secret).

**The four elements: benefit, intrigue, specificity, credibility.** The Makepeace Makeover in three steps: "How to profit from stocks in 2006" (flat, benefit only) → "How to make 307% profit from gold stocks in 2006" (specificity + implied research) → "How to exploit Warren Buffett's 'Bull Market X-Factor' and make 307% profits on gold stocks in the next 60 days" (benefit + intrigue + credibility + deadline).

**Specificity is the key ingredient.** A precise number implies research; a named mechanism differentiates your claim from identical-sounding ones and makes it believable.

**Reveal the mechanism.** The means, method, or system the prospect uses to get the benefit. Mega-powerful in saturated, skeptical markets because it introduces benefit, credibility, and intrigue at once.

**Vary length, blindness, and rhythm.** Some long, some short, some blind, some revealing the full usable secret. Reading the run should feel like a roller coaster; he shouldn't know which way he'll be turned next.

**Size doesn't matter, seduction does.** "It's not about how big or complex your bullets are, it's about how believably they seduce and build DESIRE."

**Placement:** best bullets go in the most important real estate: front cover, deck copy, back cover (5–10 teaser bullets that force him to open), sidebars, premium descriptions, and to enhance the guarantee presentation. PLUS bullets close a list.

**Sequenced bullets.** Run bullets where each feeds off the last around a single secret. No one bullet must carry full specificity and proof, as long as the section supplies it elsewhere.

**Themed / billboarded bullets.** Group tightly connected bullets on one topic under a label such as "7 Guilty Secrets Drug Companies Do NOT Want You To Know," with FACT #1, FACT #2, so the prospect feels he's getting a thorough exposé.

**Bullets crush headline block.** Start with bullets; by headline time you have dozens or hundreds of candidates. Bottom Line Personal's control headline was harvested from hundreds of written bullets: **"How Doctors Stay Well While Treating Sick People All Day."**

**When you have no secret to hide:** write pure benefit sequences. Make the benefit clear, genuinely desired, and carried by strong verbs: *deadened, rejuvenated, sparking, revived, electrified, renewed, revitalized*. Or invert into horrifying alternatives ("Ages your eyes…", "Taxes your brain…").

**Colloquial expressions** say much more with fewer words.

**Build a bullet archive** and add to it whenever you see a great example.

### What this warns against

- **Flat, institutional bullets** with a generic benefit and no specificity. The vagueness makes even the benefit unbelievable.
- **Mediocre filler.** Three of four bullets being merely okay is the tell of a writer who didn't write 7x what he needed. A few bad bullets alone can get the promotion thrown out.
- **Dull or repetitive runs.**
- **Overusing "secret."** If everything is a secret, each one is less unique and skepticism rises.
- **Overusing "sneaky."** Not everything is a conspiracy.
- **Warning bullets without proof of a solution.**
- **"Single most" claims without proof.**
- **Exclamation marks.** They often decrease response because they smell like hype.
- **Writing bullets last.** Doing so forfeits the hundreds of headline candidates the process generates.
- **Chasing length or complexity** instead of believable desire.
