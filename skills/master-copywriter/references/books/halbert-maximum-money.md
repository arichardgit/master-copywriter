## How To Make Maximum Money In Minimum Time — Gary C. Halbert

**Best for:** Direct-mail and direct-response copy that has to look and sound like a personal letter from a real human being, plus the list, offer, and format decisions that decide whether the copy ever gets read.

**Core thesis:** Advertising is "salesmanship multiplied," not creativity, and the single greatest cause of failure is that the message never gets delivered, seen, or opened. Halbert's whole method is to remove every signal that says "this is an ad" — personal envelope, article-format ad, memo-style letter, everyday English — and to aim that message at a list of people who already have the passion or the problem. Get the right list, make the right offer, sound like a friend, and rave with real passion about something you're genuinely proud to sell.

### Laws and principles

**Write every piece as if your life depends on it.** His origin exercise: *"Gary, what would you do if you had to make your next mailing work? What if you could only mail one letter and, if you didn't get a response, you would, quite literally, be beheaded?"*

**The A-pile vs. B-pile test.** Everyone divides mail into two piles: the A-pile (letters that are, or appear to be, personal) and the B-pile (bills, catalogs, brochures, obvious sales envelopes). *"Everybody always opens all of their A-pile mail and only some of their B-pile mail."* So: get your letter into the A-pile, and keep it there. A-pile envelopes carry a live stamp, no teaser, typed or handwritten address, plain #10, nothing oversized. Apply the same test to email subject lines, DMs, and packaging today.

**Kill the "Oh, yuk!" reaction.** An A-pile envelope that opens onto brochures, YES/NO tokens, and four-color inserts gets thrown away anyway. Let the reader find a letter and nothing else. Put the brochure, order card, and reply envelope inside a second sealed envelope marked *"Please open this envelope as soon as you have read my letter. Thanks. GH"*

**Timing is the whole seduction.** *"Selling is like seduction."* Ask too early and you're a creep; warm the prospect first and the same ask converts. This is why the sales letter precedes the order form, in every medium.

**Getting seen precedes getting read.** In newspapers: *"Your first job is not to get your customer to read your ad. No. Your first job is to get him to see your ad!"* Hence POSITION, POSITION, POSITION: front page of a section is best, back page second.

**Make it look like editorial, never like an ad.** *"Editorial material (or material that appears to be editorial) gets 500% more readership than material that is obviously advertising."* Be the reporter who fell in love with your product and wrote the rave article. No line art, no arrows, no reverse type, no award-winning graphics.

**Subheads are little ads for your ad.** A headline is *"AN AD FOR YOUR AD."* Because papers arrive folded in half, pepper the lower half with bold subheads.

**The list beats everything.** *"The most important element of all is the mailing list. It doesn't matter how attractive your offer or how brilliant your sales letter if you mail to the wrong list you're going to flop."* And: *"aside from making the wrong offer to the wrong list, not getting your letter delivered or opened is the Numero Uno mistake."* Find the crowd that is already hungry.

**Hot lists vs. cold lists.** Compiled lists (residents, occupations, car make/model, homeowners, boat owners) are for geographic and demographic customization. **Buyer lists — people who have already spent money by mail on something adjacent — are "usually much more responsive."** Spend real time in the list directories: *"I strongly suggest you get a copy... and spend about 20 hours just reading all the various listings."*

**Research before writing, always.** Halbert's ritual: go to the library and the biggest bookstore, pull the subject guides and periodical directories; check out and buy the books; photocopy the articles; clip the magazines. *"Now, lock yourself in a room and start reading."* Then alternate: market material, then your headline swipe file, then market material, then headlines, until the *"aha experience"* fires. *"You do have a collection of headlines, don't you?"*

**Sell something you love.** *"SELL SOMETHING YOU LOVE!"* is the cornerstone. List everything you enjoy, then check those passions against list availability and magazine circulations to confirm a reachable market exists.

**Passion is the missing ingredient.** *"Passion is the #1 most missing ingredient in advertising today."* Quoting Hopkins: *"People will not be bored in print!"*

**Detail sells.** *"People who are not interested in your product or service don't want to know anything about it but, those who are interested, want to know everything about it."* Rule: *"WHEN YOU ARE SELLING BY MAIL THEY WANT TO KNOW ALL YOU CAN TELL!"*

**Admit the flaws.** *"When you admit to (and describe) your shortcomings it makes you more believable when you talk up your good points."*

**Credential the promotion.** *"The #1 reason people do not buy from you is they are simply not interested in your offer. However, once you overcome that first hurdle, the #2 reason they don't buy from you is that they are not completely convinced you are for real."* A celebrity or authority doesn't persuade, it validates.

**Deliver on the promise before you ask.** In free recorded messages and lead-generation copy, give the real tip first. *"When you promise an inside secret, you must deliver. Don't ask them to call you at another number to get what they have already paid for. That's sleazy."*

### Frameworks and formulas

**The A-pile package.** Plain white #10 → live first-class postage stamp → typed or handwritten address → no teaser copy → corner card showing only a return address → inside, a plain typed letter and nothing else → a second sealed envelope holding brochure, order form and reply envelope, opened only after the letter.

**"Can It And Clone It," turning your spoken pitch into copy:**
1. Record your live phone sales calls (with permission) until you forget the recorder is running and you're at concert pitch.
2. Transcribe it; number every paragraph (a paragraph = one idea).
3. Score each paragraph 1–10 for favorable influence on the prospect.
4. Scissor them apart into three piles: **benefits**, **interesting facts**, and **everything that doesn't advance the sale**.
5. Throw pile three away. Rank the rest 10 down; throw out everything rated 5 or below.
6. Rewrite lightly, removing the "ers" and "ums." *Don't change them much.*
7. Now write it as a **long memo** to one visualized prospect: "Here are seven interesting facts about this boat… There are many benefits… here are five of the main ones."

**The postcard + free recorded message machine.** Postcard or classified teases a benefit → prospect calls a 24-hour recorded message → the message genuinely delivers useful information → the message invites the call or visit. It works because: you offer free info they want; a recording removes the threat of a high-pressure salesperson; you actually deliver, so you seem like a swell guy; you offer a hassle-free solution.

**The three S's for a cable or infomercial show:** a **Star** (authority or marquee celebrity), a **Story** (a told-in-interview transformation), a **Solution** (the most important S).

**Newspaper summary:** A. Run full pages on the front or back of a section. B. Make the ad look like an article. C. Pepper it with subheads, especially below the fold. D. Buy cheap: standby and remnant rates, offered by letter with the check enclosed.

**Yellow Pages formula:** (1) offer news; (2) offer a solution; (3) if possible, offer something free, and typeset it as a news story.

**Read-aloud tests.** Always read copy aloud to find glitches and bottlenecks. Better: read it to real prospects. "You write real good" = a loser. *"Gee, I'd like to have that book. Can you get me a copy?"* = a winner. "Run that by me one more time?" = your sentences are too long.

### Voice and craft notes

**He writes a letter to one person, not an ad to a market.** "Dear Friend" is the default salutation, and the whole piece behaves like correspondence: a signature block, a title under it ("In-print Salesman," "Ex-Con," "A Caring Father," "Kindness Personified"), and stacked P.S., P.P.S., P.P.P.S. sections carrying the offer, a bonus idea, or a joke.

**Sentences are short and unafraid of fragments.** He'll write a full paragraph as one word: "True." "Onward." "Whatever." "Bingo!" "It's so sad." "Case closed. End of lecture." A long explanatory sentence is followed by a three-word verdict. That alternation is the engine of his momentum.

**Constant second-person interrogation.** He asks the reader questions and answers them himself: "Did it work? I'd say so." "So how successful was that ad? Gosh, I thought you'd never ask!" "Hey, you still with me? You are? Good." "Want more? I knew you did." "Am I right? Aha, I thought so."

**Transitions are spoken cues, not connectives.** "Now listen:" "Hark unto me." "Come closer. Listen:" "Onward." "Press on. Press on. Press on." "But wait." "Hold the phone." "So anyway…" These re-grab attention every few hundred words.

**Typography carries emphasis instead of adverbs.** Big centered ALL-CAPS lines drop into the middle of running text as mini-headlines, often broken across several short lines for rhythm. He builds to these caps lines: three or four ordinary sentences, then the payoff shouted.

**Ellipses create the pause before the reveal.** "And, what I decided I wanted my guy to see when he opened that envelope was a (gulp!)…a…a…a… A LETTER!"

**Self-deprecation plus self-mythology.** He is simultaneously "a desperate nerd from Ohio," "semi-handsome," and "Sir Gary of Halbert, Ace of Space, Prince of Print, the Ravin' Maven of Marketing." The bragging is defused by the mockery, so the proof claims land as fun rather than boasting.

**He proves with numbers and with stories, never with adjectives.** 7,300,000 cash-with-order customers. 20,000 checks a day. $96,000 extra per month from 45 minutes' work. 23 drafts of one page. Specificity is the credibility.

**Digressions are deliberate.** Every anecdote is a slow warm-up to a marketing point, and he flags the return: "But so what? What ramifications, if any, does all this have to do with our marketing efforts? Plenty."

**Everyday English, on purpose.** Clichés are a feature. Contractions everywhere. Slang. Nothing that requires re-reading.

### What this book warns against

- **Bulk-rate look, label addressing, teaser copy, printed indicia,** "the stupidest mistake in direct mail," and any envelope that announces a commercial message.
- **Graphics-on-parade packages:** brochures, YES/NO tokens, windows, tear strips. They trigger "Oh, yuk!"
- **Trying to be creative or clever.** "FORGET TRYING TO BE CREATIVE! FORGET TRYING TO BE CLEVER! STOP WORRYING ABOUT THOSE AWARDS YOU'D LIKE TO WIN!"
- **Image advertising and the "years-to-come" cop-out.** "People who can't produce results often get very creative when it comes to creating cop-outs."
- **Selling something you're ashamed of.** Money made without self-respect brings "a dry rot of the psyche."
- **Vagueness and generality.** "A few vapid generalities" won't sell anything bought sight unseen.
- **Long sentences, jargon, and copy you never read aloud.**
- **Suppressing passion to avoid offending a handful of people who were never going to buy.**
- **Running an ad inside a section instead of the front or back.**
- **Teasing a secret and then withholding it** until the prospect pays or calls again.
- **Believing your own PR** ("this can be fatal"), lying to customers, and rubbing wealth in people's faces.
- **The operational sins:** not acknowledging orders immediately, filling orders slowly, delaying refunds, ignoring customer mail, rolling out faster than you can fulfill. "There is nothing subjective about an unfilled order."
