## The Adweek Copywriting Handbook — Joseph Sugarman

**Best for:** Long-form direct-response copy that must hold a cold reader from headline to order button — print ads, sales letters, VSL and infomercial scripts, long web pages.

**Core thesis:** Copywriting is a mental process, not a writing trick: you load your brain with general and specific knowledge, let it incubate, then pour it out and edit it down. The single job of copy is to get the reader to read the *next* line. Every element exists only to pull the reader deeper, down a "slippery slide" they cannot climb back out of. Along that slide you build a buying environment, get the reader nodding yes, sell a *concept* rather than a product, and trip a stack of psychological triggers. The goal never changes: "to cause a person to exchange his or her hard-earned money for a product or service."

### Laws and principles

**The purpose of every element.** Axiom 2: "All the elements in an advertisement are primarily designed to do one thing and one thing only: get you to read the first sentence of the copy." Headline, subhead, photo, caption, logo, price, layout. None of them exist to convey benefits. They exist to deliver the reader to sentence one.

**The purpose of the headline** is *not* to state a benefit. It is to get you to read the subheadline, which gets you to the first sentence. Sugarman's favorite headlines are short and curiosity-loaded: "Vision Breakthrough," "Pocket CB," "Magic Baloney." Subheads run around 16 words.

**First sentence / second sentence.** Axiom 3: "The sole purpose of the first sentence in an advertisement is to get you to read the second sentence." Make it short, almost not a sentence. No multisyllabic words. "Keep it short, sweet and almost incomplete so that the reader has to read the next sentence." Think of a locomotive: the hardest work is the first foot of movement. Do not put the sales pitch at the beginning; Sugarman tried every trick to disprove this and failed each time.

**The slippery slide.** Axiom 6: "Your readers should be so compelled to read your copy that they cannot stop reading until they read all of it as if sliding down a slippery slide." Picture a playground slide greased with baby oil. Every element must add momentum. Once a reader passes 25% of your ad, there is a high probability they finish it.

**The greased chute, seeds of curiosity.** Plant a short teaser at the end of a paragraph that forces the reader into the next one, especially where the copy naturally slows. Use them; don't overdo them. Bigger seeds work too: promise a payoff early that is only revealed at the very end. Axiom 8: "Keep the copy interesting and the reader interested through the power of curiosity."

**Create the buying environment.** Axiom 4: "Your ad layout and the first few paragraphs of your ad must create the buying environment most conducive to the sale." The Honolulu art gallery led him into a carpeted back room, dimmed lights, classical music, two spotlights on the painting, and he nearly bought. In print you *control* the environment completely, through word choice, phrasing, layout and the level of integrity you convey. Discount product → busy graphics, big prices. Expensive product → class, restraint, confidence.

**Resonate, get the yes.** Axiom 5: "Get the reader to say yes and harmonize with your accurate and truthful statements while reading your copy." The moment the reader says "no," "I don't believe that," or "that doesn't apply to me," you have lost them.

**Emotion vs. logic.** Three emotion principles: (1) Every word has an emotion associated with it and tells a story. (2) Every good ad is an emotional outpouring of words, feelings and impressions. (3) **You sell on emotion, but you justify a purchase with logic.** Nobody buys a Mercedes for the braking system; they buy to belong, then recite the technical features to friends. A phrase need not be logically correct if it lands emotionally: "a prompt and courteous refund" makes no logical sense and works beautifully. "The old woman in the motel" became "the little old lady in the cottage." Caples changed *repair* to *fix* and gained 20%.

**Sell the concept, not the product.** Axiom 9: "Never sell a product or service. Always sell a concept." A smoke detector became "The Nose." A walkie-talkie on channel 12 became the Pocket CB. A chess computer became the "Soviet Challenge." The exception: when a product is so new the product itself *is* the concept. Price also changes concept: the same CB was a serious radio at $39.95, a walkie-talkie at $29.95, a toy at $19.95.

**Long enough but short enough.** Axiom 11: "Copy should be long enough to cause the reader to take the action you request." Copy length is a non-issue; compellingness is the issue. Two things drive length up: a high price point, and an unusual item. Educating the reader on intrinsic value is equivalent to lowering the price.

**Be personal.** Axiom 12: "Every communication should be a personal one, from the writer to the recipient, regardless of the medium used." Use *I*, *me*, *you*. Use a byline.

**Flow and anticipation.** Axiom 13: ideas should "flow in a logical fashion, anticipating your prospect's questions and answering them as if the questions were asked face-to-face."

**Editing.** Axiom 14: "In the editing process, you refine your copy to express exactly what you want to express with the fewest words." Fewer words = a less imposing ad = a shorter, faster, more slippery slide.

**Other axioms:** Axiom 1 — copywriting is a mental process reflecting the sum of your experiences and specific knowledge. Axiom 7 — don't assume constraints that aren't really there. Axiom 10 — the incubation process is your subconscious solving the problem. Axiom 15 — **sell a cure, not a preventive**, unless you can position the preventive as a cure. You'll pay $20 to prevent a disease and $1,000 to cure it.

### The 31 psychological triggers

1. **Involvement/Ownership** — make the reader mentally turn the knobs. "Take a ride in the new Corvette. Feel the breeze…" Involvement devices double and triple response. 2. **Honesty** — the single most important point; consumers detect what you covered up. Naming your product's flaws up front sells more. 3. **Integrity** — reflected in layout, typeface, restraint. 4. **Credibility** — explain *why* your price is low; piggyback on the medium's credibility, brand names, celebrity, company name. 5. **Value and Proof of Value** — prove it by comparison; education is value. 6. **Justify the Purchase** — resolve "can I afford this?" with savings, health, recognition, tax angles, or "you deserve it." 7. **Greed** — bargains motivate; too-low prices need justifying. 8. **Establish Authority** — "America's largest single source of space-age products." 9. **Satisfaction Conviction** — go past a trial period to something that makes readers think "how can they do it?" Changing only this doubled response in one test. 10. **Nature of Product** — every product has one dramatic, true way to be presented. 11. **Nature of Prospect** — know their emotional needs. 12. **Current Fads** — ride them early, exit right after the peak. 13. **Timing** — nobody knows; that's why you test. 14. **Linking** — bridge the new to something already understood ("The Nose," "Pocket CB," "vitamins for your car," "tune-up in a pill"). 15. **Consistency** — make the first commitment tiny; once committed, people buy consistently with it (upsells convert >50%). 16. **Harmonize** — meet the market where it is. 17. **Desire to Belong** — Mercedes, Marlboro, Volvo owners buy into a group. 18. **Desire to Collect** — your best list for watches is watch owners. 19. **Curiosity** — the single strongest motivator in mail order. Never show the view through the BluBlockers. Show too much and you kill your only advantage. 20. **Sense of Urgency** — limited editions, closeouts, ship-by dates, but never a lie. 21. **Fear** — of loss, crime, illness, aging, missing out. 22. **Instant Gratification** — beat retail by shipping fast and saying so. 23. **Exclusivity, Rarity, Uniqueness** — the 100-mph snowmobile he didn't need and bought because only six existed in the state. 24. **Simplicity** — one offer, no choices. One watch outpulled nine watches 3-to-1 in an A/B split. 25. **Human Relationships** — every element is a tuning fork that must resonate; use hands, faces, humor, bylines. 26. **Storytelling** — "selling is a matter of capturing people's attention and holding it with a good story." 27. **Mental Engagement** — "The more the mind must work to reach a conclusion that it eventually successfully reaches, the more positive, enjoyable, or stimulating the experience." Leave the punch line unsaid. 28. **Guilt** — free gifts, and simply giving so much value the reader feels obliged. 29. **Specificity** — not "dentists everywhere recommend" but "92 percent of new dentists"; not "a lot of nerve endings" but "72,000 nerve endings." 30. **Familiarity** — never kill a campaign because *you* are tired of it; kill it when sales stop. 31. **Hope** — imply a future benefit; never promise a measurable result you can't deliver.

### Frameworks and formulas

**The 10 graphic elements:** Headline, Subheadline, Photo/Drawing, Caption, Copy, Paragraph Headings, Logo, Price, Response Device, Overall Layout. All ten serve one purpose: get the first sentence read.

**The 23 copy elements (use as a checklist):** 1. Typeface (serif, a 5-to-1 comprehension advantage) 2. First Sentence 3. Second Sentence 4. Paragraph Headings (their real job is to make copy look less intimidating) 5. Product Explanation (explain a complicated product simply, a simple product complexly) 6. New Features 7. Technical Explanation (builds expert trust even when the reader can't follow it) 8. Anticipate Objections 9. Resolve Objections 10. Gender 11. Clarity 12. Clichés (avoid) 13. Rhythm (vary sentence length; use triads) 14. Service 15. Physical Facts (a missing fact is an excuse not to buy) 16. Trial Period (one month minimum; longer trials mean fewer returns) 17. Price Comparison 18. Testimonials (including "reverse testimonials") 19. Price 20. Offer Summary 21. Avoid Saying Too Much 22. Ease of Ordering 23. Ask for the Order.

**Seven steps to writing great copy:** 1. Become an expert on the product. 2. Know your prospect. 3. Write your headline and subheadline. 4. Write the copy, no worry about grammar, just dump everything. 5. Edit, cut every unnecessary word. 6. Incubate, walk away. 7. Take a final look. Repeat 5 and 6 as needed.

**The copy sequence (block diagram).** Break the ad into abbreviated copy blocks that flow in one direction, down. For the Bally pinball ad: excitement/environment → how it's different → construction and quality → justification of the purchase → lasting play value → service → offer → ask. Each block answers the question the previous block provoked.

**Concept generation:** state the problem, restate it differently, restate it again, list several big ideas, pick one or two, visualize, then incubate.

**Patterning:** take an admired ad for a similar product and use its structure as a scaffold, without copying the layout.

**Fog Index (Gunning):** take 100–125 words → average sentence length + percentage of three-syllable words × 0.4 = grade level. "Vision Breakthrough" scored 4.9. Best-sellers run 8th–10th grade; WSJ 11th.

**Editing principles:** kill "that" clauses; edit for rhythm; combine sentences; delete unnecessary articles; rearrange thoughts for better flow. A 59-word paragraph became 36 with identical meaning.

### Swipe: verbatim lines

1. "I am about to tell you a true story. If you believe me, you will be well rewarded. If you don't believe me, I will make it worth your while to change your mind. Let me explain."
2. Headline: "Magic Baloney" / Subhead: "You'll love the way we hated the Magic Stat thermostat until an amazing thing happened." / Caption: "It had no digital readout, an ugly case and a stupid name. It almost made us sick."
3. "You're probably expecting our typical sales pitch, but get ready for a shock. For instead of trying to tell you what a great product the Magic Stat thermostat is, we're going to tear it apart. Unmercifully."
4. Headline: "HOT" / Subhead: "A new consumer concept lets you buy stolen merchandise if you're willing to take a risk."
5. "We developed an exciting new consumer marketing concept. It's called 'stealing.' That's right, stealing!"
6. "Consumers are being robbed. Inflation is stealing our purchasing power. Our dollars are shrinking in value."
7. "So that's our concept. We recycle 'lousy rotten' garbage into super new products with five-year warranties. We steal from the rich manufacturers and give to the poor consumer."
8. "I'm a farmer. And the story I tell you is the absolute truth, as incredible as it may seem." (Frank Schultz, "Fluke of Nature")
9. "I used to work hard. The 18-hour days. The 7-day weeks. But I didn't start making big money until I did less — a lot less." (Joe Karbo)
10. "What's more, I'm going to ask you to send me 10 dollars for something that'll cost me no more than 50 cents. And I'll try to make it so irresistible that you'd be a darned fool not to do it."
11. "Most people are too busy earning a living to make any money."
12. "You're stuck. You're at a phone booth trying to find a phone number, and people are waiting. You feel the pressure."
13. "We all take our food supply for granted. And for good reason. Americans have always had plenty. But we may be heading for one of the most serious periods in our history. Let me explain."
14. "If I were to buy a ticket in the Illinois State Lottery, my chances of winning would be a million to one. But if I were to bet that you, as a reader of this publication, have high blood pressure and don't even know it, my chances of being correct would be eight to one."
15. "But what if you never buy from us and your two-year membership expires? Fine. Send us just your membership card and we'll fully refund your five dollars plus send you interest on your money."
16. "If you aren't absolutely satisfied, return your product within 30 days for a prompt and courteous refund."
17. "Beauty is only skin deep and a name doesn't really mean that much. But we sure wish those guys at Magic Stat would have named their unit something more impressive. Maybe something like Twinkle Temp."
18. Short first sentences: "Losing weight is not easy." / "It's you against a computer." / "It's easy." / "It had to happen." / "Hats off to IBM."
19. Seeds of curiosity: "But there's more." / "So read on." / "But I didn't stop there." / "Let me explain." / "Now here comes the good part."
20. "Order a pair or two at no obligation, today."

### Voice and craft notes

Sugarman writes like a smart, slightly wry friend leaning across a table. First person, byline, contractions, plain words. Sentences vary deliberately — short, long, medium, short, short, then one long one — because monotony kills. He uses triads constantly ("quality, accuracy, ruggedness"). Paragraphs are short and broken by all-caps headings that exist mostly to make the column look easy. He starts with a story or a clipped news oddity, often unrelated, and bridges it to the product ("Now you probably wonder what dirty British underwear has to do with this new pocket-sized computer. I'm glad you asked."). He answers questions one beat *before* the reader asks them.

On proof: technical explanation the reader can't fully follow, given honestly, builds trust in the expert. Specific numbers beat adjectives. On objections: raise the ugliest one yourself, in your own words, then dismantle it. On closes: summarize the offer, ask for the order, add a satisfaction conviction so generous the reader wonders how you survive it, then a truthful sense of urgency, then "today."

### What this book warns against

Never open with the sales pitch. Never use clichés. Never write logic-first for an emotional purchase. Never use scare tactics as a substitute for understanding a product's nature. Never resolve an objection you haven't first named. Never omit a physical fact, however trivial: "give readers any excuse not to buy and they won't buy." Never offer choices in a direct-response ad; simplicity beats selection 3-to-1. Never say too much; over-explaining robs the reader's mind of the pleasure of reaching the conclusion. Never make a specific, measurable promise when the trigger is hope. Never lie for urgency at the end and blow the integrity you spent a thousand words building. Never mail big without testing. Never sell a preventive when you could position a cure. Never drop a working campaign because you're bored of it. Never let assumed constraints decide what you attempt. And never forget the failure modes at the end of the ad: Victoria's Secret wrote a beautiful ad and lost money by omitting a return policy and an incentive at the exact moment the buying decision was made.
