## Take Their Money: How To Write Words That REALLY Make You Rich — Kyle Milligan

**Best for:** Decoding and writing long-form direct-response sales letters (Agora-style promos) by reading copy for emotional subtext rather than filling in templates.

**Core thesis:** Copywriting is a foreign language communicated purely through subtext: "it's not what you say, it's what you communicate." Four emotions — New, Easy, Safe, Big — each with an amplifying alter ego, sit beneath every line of every winning promo. Once you can translate any line into which emotion it's firing, you can stop hunting for templates and generate the words yourself.

### Laws and principles

- **It's not what you say, it's what you communicate.** Judge every line by the emotion it plants, not its cleverness.
- **Don't read about copy, read copy.** Fluency comes from binging the language itself, daily, with a decoder in hand.
- **Practice active reading, never passive.** Read pen in hand, marking which of the Big 4 each line fires and what objection it answers.
- **Run The Daily Practice** (from Joe Schriefer): read a piece of copy a day, write a page of copy a day, come up with an idea a day. The idea needn't be a big idea, just stay in the habit of asking "what if…"
- **Categorization is DEATH.** The moment the lizard brain can say "I've seen this before," you are buried. Novelty isn't decoration, it's survival.
- **New is the source of hope.** Your prospect has already tried and failed with something similar and blames themselves. New says: *this time could be different.* That hope, not intrigue, is New's real power.
- **New and different beats "better."** A better version of a known thing is still a known thing.
- **Write to the lazy and the insecure.** Nobody likes work; anybody who loves work isn't reading your copy, they're working. Reduce workload (laziness) and reduce required qualifications (insecurity).
- **Safe does double duty.** It lowers skepticism *and* raises perceived value: people judge risky things as less valuable. Given an 80% shot at $100 versus a guaranteed $80, most take the $80. Sell certainty at a premium.
- **Never claim 100%.** A perfect claim creates more skepticism, not less. Put a believable number on it (93% certainty), a numerical Safe.
- **Let the reader predict the ending.** Set up a historical pattern and let them infer what happens next; then they are selling themselves.
- **Intrigue alone is not useful.** WIIFM is everyone's favorite radio station. A headline that only teases fails.
- **Go as ultra-specific as legally possible, and don't lie.** $10,251 beats $10,000; odd numbers work best. Specificity implies the claim has already been tested. Base it on an example you will actually demonstrate later.
- **The headline, lead, and body share a "Soul,"** one sentence summing up the whole promo (elsewhere called the Golden Thread or the Rule of One). Anything deviating from it gets deleted. Trump Bonus Checks ran on: *"Trump did a thing, now you get paid."*
- **Get to the Big Promise as soon as possible.** As. Soon. As. Possible.
- **Proof comes in threes.** One or two pieces feel unresolved. If no single proof point hits your claim, ladder three ascending proofs up to it.
- **Show, don't tell. "If you are telling, you aren't selling."** Never use raw data as proof; the brain cannot reach a motivated conclusion from facts, it needs story.
- **Reframe every proof two to three times.** A one-time $10,000 win becomes a six-figure yearly income becomes 765x the S&P.
- **Never divulge the secret.** If the reader thinks they've figured it out, they leave, to do it alone or buy a cheaper version.
- **Rename everything.** Not a report but a Dossier, Bulletin, Hot Sheet, Prospectus. Not a phone number but "a dedicated team of specialists for this project."
- **Testimonials must mirror the reader** in appearance, economic status, and location, and must be long and specific.
- **Price anchor ceiling: roughly 20x the actual price.** $8,414 down to $49 reads as bullshit; $421 down to $49 lands.
- **Ask for the sale; don't close.** Instruct without commanding. Choice = autonomy. Nobody likes to be preached at.
- **Urgency is hot sauce, not a food group.** The Big 4 is the meal. Small doses throughout; heavy pour in the offer section only.

### Frameworks and formulas

**The Big 4 (mnemonic: NESB), with alter egos as amplifiers**
- **New / Only** — signal words: Breakthrough, Revolutionary, Discovery, Game Changing, Disruptive, NEW, Announcing, Revealed, Breaking; Only: Exclusive, Patented, Only here, Proprietary.
- **Easy / Anybody** — Easy, Anybody, Simple, 1-2-3, Push-button, Automatic, Fool-proof, Step-by-step, System, "All you have to do is…", "You don't need…"
- **Safe / Predictable** — Happened before, Predictable, Safe, History is repeating itself, Just like we've seen before, "The last time this happened…"
- **Big / Fast** — Big, Fast, Monster, Huge, Once in a lifetime, Quickly, Overnight, Weekly. Big pairs naturally with FOMO.

**Not Statements** — two uses: (1) build intrigue ("It's not muscle confusion. It's not power lifting. And it's not cardio…"), (2) convey Easy/Anybody by knocking out disqualifying hurdles line by line, leaving the reader over-qualified.

**The Four Sections of Copy:** 1) Headline, 2) Lead, 3) Body, 4) Offer. Order matters.

**The Headline Complex:** eyebrow (e.g., "WARNING: Limited Spots Available") → headline → deck copy → testimonial box.

**The 4 U's (Mark Ford):** Urgent, Unique, Useful, Ultra-Specific. **Unique is non-negotiable** (a synonym for New). Urgency and Ultra-Specific are the droppable ones. If two or more are missing the headline won't hold attention.

**Finding Unique:** pull one item from your offer and emphasize it. The Money Zone doesn't trade traditional stocks; Night Trader trades at night. Thinking hack: "The first ______ to ever…"

**Three ways to build a USP:** (1) have something actually unique (proprietary/patented), (2) label something common as unique (Sugarman's gold-plated fire-alarm connectors), (3) give something old a new name (Modified Carbohydrate Diet → South Beach Diet; MP3 player → "1,000 songs in your pocket").

**The Lead, five jobs:** capture attention (often via a **pattern interrupt**), expand upon the headline (a **payoff**, a little more detail without giving anything away), establish credibility (one line is enough here), make the Big Promise, open a loop (Makepeace's "**bribe**"). A lead is the abstract of a scholarly article: everything compelling, compressed. Typically 380–500 words, sometimes 2–7 pages. The lead ends when the guru is introduced.

**Open-loop mechanics:** "In just a second, I will show you exactly how…" Easiest reliable move: open a new loop immediately before closing the previous one ("But before we dive into…").

**The Body Template (the one template to always follow): Objection → Claim → Proof → Benefit.** Generate objections by running the Big 4 against your promise: New → "Why am I just now hearing about this?" / "Why should I buy this from you?"; Easy → "Do I need experience?" / "What if I don't have money?"; Safe → "What guarantees do I have?" / "How do I know this will work?"; Big → "How much can I make?" / "How fast?"

**Common objections list:** Why not use an alternative? Why act now? Who are you? Why listen to you? How did you figure this out? How does it work? Why are you telling me? Is this difficult? What do I need to start? Will it take time? Is it risky? How big are the results? How fast? How much does it cost? What if it doesn't work?

**Seven types of proof:** case studies, demonstrations, testimonials, quotes from authorities, charts and graphs, official documents, articles and headlines.

**Benefits: "Imagine…"** then paint the scene. Then delete "imagine" and state it flatly to avoid echo.

**The Seven Deadly Benefits:** what people consider most personal is most universal. Mine lust, gluttony, greed, sloth, wrath, envy, pride. People buy to stick it to the smug brother-in-law, the controlling father, the teacher who said they'd amount to nothing.

**Named body-copy sections:** *Better Than Section* (sell them out of the hot thing of the moment: "That's Even Better Than Bitcoin"); *The Last Time Something Like This Happened* (historical pattern → Predictability; Halbert's Water Ad is the model); *Guru/Credibility Section* (the hard line where lead becomes body); *Discovery Story* (answers "how do you know about this?" → New/Only); *Key Insight* (the one fact the reader must believe, proved with "signs of things to come"); *Layman's Explanation* (what one half-drunk guy at a bar says to convince his skeptical friend); *Technical Explanation* (the Layman's in a lab coat, jargon so dense the reader concedes "you're smarter than me, trust me" → Safe).

**The Offer section (last 20–30%), in order:** summarize everything so far (rebuild the lead's hype, no new claims) → intro the report → intro the service (USP → how it works / what to expect / what you get → pump in some cred) → add bonuses (always related to the core opportunity) → testimonials → recap → guarantee → limit spots or quantity → set a deadline → ask for the sale.

**Front end vs. back end:** front end ≤ ~$50, built to acquire a buyer, usually carries a trial offer and money-back guarantee; back end $2,000–$10,000+, built for profit, usually no refund.

**The Price Anchor:** bullet each deliverable with a teased detail, assign each a dollar value, total them, then reveal the price and reframe the discount multiple ways ("over 80% off… just 13 cents a day… a price anyone could afford"). Alternative anchor: **apples to oranges**, comparing a $2,000 newsletter to $250,000 in hedge-fund fees.

**Guarantees without refunds:** guarantee *results* or continued work. "We guarantee the chance to see 10x your money or we'll work for free." Note the careful wording: the *chance* to see 10x.

**Gathering testimonials:** decide the exact story you want, then ask 3–5 open-ended leading questions that walk the customer into telling it.

**Urgency vs. Scarcity:** Urgency = "get in by this date." Scarcity = "get in by this date… if you still can." Scarcity limits quantity and triggers exclusion, so it files under New's alter ego "Only."

**The Crossroads Close:** "You now have two choices. You can choose to join me… Or you can click away and go back to the life you've always had." Two options only.

**P.S.:** an abbreviated re-run of the lead, placed right after the sign-off.

### Voice and craft notes

Short lines. One thought per line. Heavy ellipses to pull the eye down the page… then a hard period for the landing. Second person, contractions, mild profanity, pop-culture analogies that make abstractions physical. He teaches the way he sells: opens loops on himself, tells you he'll show you something "in just a second," then delivers.

Proof is handled as escalating narrative, never as a data dump: three cases, each reframed into a second and third perspective (per-month, per-year, versus the index). Credibility is rationed: one line in the lead, a dedicated Guru section in the body, testimonials and track record in the offer. Objections are answered *before* the reader forms them, usually by turning the answer into a subhead so the objection itself is never spoken aloud. Technical detail is deployed not to inform but to induce deference. Everything sells; nothing merely explains.

### What this book warns against

- **Templates and fill-in-the-blank swipes.** They give you one sentence for one moment and no system for generating the next.
- **Reading books about copywriting instead of reading copy.**
- **Being "conversational" as a goal.** Conversational English isn't copywriting; it doesn't automatically create intrigue, novelty, ease, safety, or urgency.
- **Letting the reader categorize you.** Any whiff of "this is like that thing I saw yesterday" is fatal.
- **Selling "better" instead of new and different.**
- **Relying on intrigue with no WIIFM.**
- **Claiming 100% certainty.**
- **Giving away the secret** in the offer section.
- **Raw data as proof.**
- **Offering only one or two proof points.**
- **Random, unrelated bonuses.**
- **Price anchors above ~20x the real price.**
- **FAQ sections.** They surrender conversation control, let the reader scan and jump, and tell after you've spent the whole letter showing.
- **Telling the reader what they must do at the close.**
- **Treating urgency as a substitute for the Big 4.** A meal of hot sauce is unswallowable.
