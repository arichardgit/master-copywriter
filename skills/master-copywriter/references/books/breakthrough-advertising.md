## Breakthrough Advertising — Eugene M. Schwartz

**Best for:** Choosing what a headline should say before you write a word of it — diagnosing a market's awareness and sophistication, picking the one mass desire to harness, and structuring long-form body copy so belief is built layer by layer.

**Core thesis:** Copy cannot create desire. Mass desire already exists in millions of people, built by social, economic and technological forces no advertiser could afford to manufacture; the copywriter's entire job is to channel that already-existing desire onto one particular product. Where you enter that desire — what your headline may and may not say — is dictated not by your product but by two facts about the market: how much it already knows (State of Awareness) and how many rival products have already claimed the same thing (State of Sophistication). Everything after the headline is the construction of belief.

### Laws and principles

- **Never fight mass desire; harness it.** "You cannot create it, and you cannot fight it. But you can — and must — direct it, channel it, focus it onto your particular product." Advertising that exploits existing desire produces an *Amplification Effect*, $1 of advertising creating $50 or $100 in sales. Advertising that tries to create desire "is no longer advertising but education," and returns at best a dollar for a dollar.
- **Measure every candidate desire on three dimensions before choosing:** *urgency* (arthritic pain vs. a minor headache), *staying power* (raw hunger vs. a craving for gourmet food), and *scope* (how many people share it). Pick the one strongest in all three right now. "If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter."
- **Sell the functional product, never the physical product.** Every product is two products: the steel/glass/paper, and "the product in action, the series of benefits." "No physical part of your product can ever become a headline." Physical facts earn their keep only afterward: to justify price, document quality, promise the performance will last, sharpen the mental picture, and above all to give the claim *a fresh new basis for believability*.
- **The headline's only job is to stop the prospect and force him to read the second sentence.** It need not sell, name the product, or even name the main appeal. Demanding a full sale from 10–20% of the ad's space "is simply to admit defeat."
- **One product yields dozens of performances but the ad can feature only one.** "Every product gives you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock."
- **Your most powerful claim is often not your most powerful headline.** Interest *and* believability determine the headline. Awareness can be redefined as *readiness to accept*.
- **The law of belief:** "If you violate your prospect's established beliefs in the slightest degree… then nothing you promise him, no matter how appealing, can save your ad." Conversely: "One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises."
- **Position increases power.** Every claim has two sources of strength: its content, and the preparation laid for it. "In copy 1 + 1 can often equal 10."
- **A new structure is a new ad.** Same content, rearranged, produces new ideas, new sharpness, new believability.
- **Prevention only sells when the danger is aimed at someone else.** "A man will not visualize future disasters occurring to himself, but he is perfectly capable of visualizing… such future problems affecting others around him." Cavity prevention sold to parents about children; life insurance sold on the widow, not the death.
- **Never attack a competitor's weakness unless you supply the cure in the same breath.**
- **You cannot repeat, but you can reinforce.** Each fresh perspective on the same promise reinforces the ones before; a repeated *phrasing* loses the reader mid-ad.
- **No formula works twice.** "Each and every formula is simply the written solution to a particular problem that occurred in the past." Replace formulas with *analysis*: "a series of measuring rods, check-points, signpost questions."

### Frameworks and formulas

**The three-stage process of channeling desire.** 1) Choose the most powerful desire applicable to your product. 2) Acknowledge, reinforce and/or offer to satisfy that desire in a single statement in the headline. 3) Show how the performances built into your product inevitably satisfy it.

**The Five States of Awareness** (the headline begins at the prospect's exact point of awareness):
1. **Most Aware** — knows the product, knows what it does, wants it. Headline = name plus bargain price. *"Revere Zomar Lens, Electric Eye Camera — Formerly $149.50 — Now Only $119.95."* "There is nothing creative about his job, and he should receive the lowest possible scale of pay."
2. **Product-Aware but not yet wanting it** — the bulk of all advertising. Product name in the headline plus its superiority. Seven tasks: (a) reinforce desire (*"Steinway — The Instrument of the Immortals"*); (b) sharpen the image of how it satisfies (*"At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in a Rolls Royce is the electric clock"*); (c) extend where/when (*"Thirst knows no season"*); (d) new proof/documentation (*"9 out of 10 screen stars use Lux Toilet Soap"*); (e) new mechanism that satisfies better (*"Hoover's new invention washes floors and vacuums up the scrub water"*); (f) new mechanism removing former limitations (*"A new Zenith hearing aid — inconspicuous beyond belief"*); (g) change the image/mechanism entirely to escape competition.
3. **Desire-Aware, product-unknown** (new products) — three steps: name the desire and/or its solution in the headline; prove the solution can be accomplished; show that the mechanism of that accomplishment is in your product. *"To men who want to quit work some day."*
4. **Need-Aware** (problem-solving) — name the need and/or its solution; dramatize the need until he feels how badly he needs the cure; present the product as the inevitable solution. *"Corns?"* … *"Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery"* … *"Do YOU make these mistakes in English?"*
5. **Completely Unaware** — the hardest. Rules of elimination: no price, no product name, no direct statement of function or desire. What's left is the market itself. Write an **identification headline**: "You are selling nothing, promising nothing, satisfying nothing. Instead, you are echoing an emotion, an attitude… You are telling them what they are." Cardinal rule: it must be *his* headline, and "it must pick out the product's logical prospects — and reject as many people as it attracts."

Fifth-stage patterns with worked examples: giving words to a hidden dream (*The University of the Night*), exploiting a hidden fear (*Why Men Crack…*), leading into an unacceptable problem via a universally accepted image (*Within the Curve of a Woman's Arm*), projecting a hidden desire that can't be spoken (Marlboro tattoo), using a common resentment (*Why Haven't TV Owners Been Told These Facts?*), projecting an ultimate triumph (*They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano*), projecting the *result* of a problem people would reject stated directly (*Often a Bridesmaid but Never a Bride*), projecting the reward instead of the work (*Here's an Extra $50, Grace*).

**The Five Stages of Market Sophistication:**
1. **First in the market** — be simple, be direct, don't be fancy. Name the need or the claim, nothing more. *"NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"*
2. **Second in** — copy the working claim but enlarge it, drive it to the limit, outbid the competition. *"LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS — OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!"* Roses: 25–50–100 blooms → *"SIX HUNDRED MUMS FROM A SINGLE BUSH!"* → *"WHO EVER HEARD OF 17,000 BLOOMS FROM A SINGLE PLANT?"*
3. **Claims exhausted** — bring in a **NEW MECHANISM**. Emphasis shifts from *what* the product does to *how* it works. The claim shrinks to shorthand and the mechanism takes the headline. *"FLOATS FAT RIGHT OUT OF YOUR BODY!"*
4. **Mechanism elaborated** — make the mechanism easier, quicker, surer; remove limitations; add benefits. *"FIRST NO-DIET REDUCING WONDER DRUG!"*
5. **Market exhausted, disbelieving** — abandon promise and mechanism for **identification**, exactly as in the Fifth State of Awareness.

**38 headline-intensification (Verbalization) devices,** used once you already have the content: measure the size of the claim; measure its speed; compare it; metaphorize it; sensitize it; demonstrate it with a prime example; dramatize it or its result; state it as a paradox; remove limitations; associate it with admired people or values; show how much work it does; state it as a question; offer how-to information; tie in authority; before-and-after it; stress newness; stress exclusivity; turn it into a challenge; state it as a case-history quotation; condense it by interchanging your product with the one it replaces; symbolize it with a parallel reality; connect the mechanism to the claim; startle by contradicting how the mechanism should work; connect need and claim; offer information in the ad itself; turn claim or need into a case history; name the problem; warn of pitfalls; emphasize by phraseology; impose a universally-overcome limitation to show ease; state the difference; surprise with limitations overcome; address the people who *can't* buy; address your prospect directly; dramatize how hard the claim was to produce; accuse the claim of being too good; challenge present limiting beliefs; turn it into question-and-answer.

**The Seven Techniques of Breakthrough Copy (body copy):**
1. **Intensification** — thirteen ways to strengthen desire: (1) present the product and satisfaction directly and in exhaustive detail; (2) put the product in action; (3) put the reader inside the action; (4) turn the demonstration into a test he performs; (5) stretch the benefits out in time; (6) bring in an audience (testimonial, narration, case history); (7) show experts astonished — "there is nothing so astounding as the astonishment of experts"; (8) compare and contrast to prove superiority; (9) picture the black side too — "You irritate the wound, and then you apply the salve that heals it"; (10) show how easy it is to get the benefits; (11) use metaphor, analogy, imagination; (12) summarize with a **catalog** (horizontal — every application; or vertical — desire piled on desire); (13) turn the **guarantee** into the climax and last summary of the ad.
2. **Identification** — build a saleable personality. Two kinds of roles: *character roles* (adjectives: chic, well-read, virile, accepted easily because never tested) and *achievement roles* (nouns/titles: Executive, $20,000-a-Year-Man, Career Woman, displayed through possessions). Method: find the product's **primary image**, then either intensify it or subordinate it and use it as a **believability-bridge** to richer images. "If you demand that your prospect jump across a believability-chasm, your ad will fail. If, however, you build a bridge… he will let you lead him almost anywhere."
3. **Gradualization** — the architecture of belief. Open with a statement accepted instantly, then chain acceptance onto acceptance until the goal-conclusion arrives as inevitable. Devices: the **Inclusion Question** ("Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?"); **Detailed Identification** (cataloguing his symptoms); **Contradiction of present false beliefs**; the **Language of Logic** ("Therefore…", "Here's why…", "The reason for this deficiency is clear…"); **Syllogistic thinking**; and belief-forms — contingency structures ("If… then…"), Repetition of Proof, Promise–Belief–Promise variation "so that the reader never has the breathing space to question," and Paragraph Parallelism.
4. **Redefinition** — remove objections by saying the product is *this* rather than *that*. Three uses: **Simplification** (repairs redefined as "minor adjustments," troubles as "warning signals"); **Escalation** (widen the benefit or make it indispensable); **Price Reduction** (switch the comparison to a more expensive standard; "You can have," not "you can buy"). The pure form is concept-judo: Lifebuoy's repellent medicinal odor turned into proof of odor-destroying power via B.O.
5. **Mechanization** — verbal proof that it works, in three stages by awareness: **name** the mechanism; **describe** it in selling language; or **feature** it as the headline claim itself. First rule: "mechanism copy… is not scientific discourse… Every word in good copy — including mechanism copy — sells." Price cuts also need a mechanism, or nobody believes them.
6. **Concentration** — destroy the alternatives. Structure A: one-for-one contrast, bad/good, bad/good. Structure B: time-sequence, what happens now with what you use, then what happens when you switch. Or compress it into a headline: *"SHRINKS HEMORRHOIDS WITHOUT SURGERY."*
7. **Camouflage** — borrow stored believability. Adopt the **format** of the medium (the Wall Street Journal ad set in Journal type, ugly line drawing and all, that ran nineteen times without drop-off); adopt its **phraseology** (dateline, byline: *"New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed…"*); adopt a non-advertising **mood** — Understatement (Volkswagen) or **Deadly Sincerity**, leaning over backwards to name flaws so the benefits land harder.

**The Final Touches:** *Verification* — proof placed where the reader unconsciously demands it. *Reinforcement* — juxtapose two claims so they multiply (Avis: "We try harder" + "We're only second"). *Interweaving* — every sentence should carry promise, logic, emotion and image at once. *Sensitivity* — feel the anticipation points where the reader says "I've read enough about this, give me this instead," and switch copy type. *Momentum* — momentum phrases ("Let me explain," "Here's how," "And yet it's only the beginning") plus deliberately incomplete statements. *Mood* — image-sharpeners vs. emotion-definers, plus rhythm: "Read the ad out loud."

### Voice and craft notes

Schwartz writes in short declaratives that accumulate, then breaks the rhythm with a fragment for emphasis. He states a principle, italicizes its formal restatement, then immediately drops a real ad on the page and dissects it phrase by phrase. He is fond of the triad ("They discover markets. They build markets. They intensify markets") and of the reversal that reframes the whole argument.

In the sample copy he prizes: physical numbers over adjectives where believability matters ("4,076 roses"; "up to 31 more horsepower"); staccato verb-first clauses for momentum; second-person future-tense scene-painting ("Next weekend you go down to your car…"); and repetition of a stem across parallel lines ("It means that… It means that… It means that…"). Objections are never argued down. They are *redefined* out of existence before the reader knows he had one. Proof is never boxed off; it is interwoven, staged to arrive at the exact moment the reader is begging for it, and written as selling copy, never as data.

### What this book warns against

- **The Word-Substitute Technique** — pulling a proven headline and swapping in your product. Produces "Echo Ads, ads that remind people of some other product."
- **Pouring your idea into a memorized formula** "like he'd pour hot lead into a mold."
- **Copying yourself.** "All this process does is bring talent down to the level of file-cabinet mediocrity."
- **Using a fifth-stage problem as an excuse for an empty headline** — a startler, a cute line, or a pretty picture with nothing behind it.
- **Piling promise on promise** to strengthen a weak ad. "What they usually get for their troubles is greater sales resistance."
- **Running a headline past its stage.** "You never step in the same river twice."
- **Reaching the outer limits of enlargement.** Claims double and triple, believability shatters, claims are automatically discounted 50%, sales fall.
- **Demanding an unbelievable identification.** Disbelief in the image spreads to the performance claims and kills the sale.
- **Contradicting a product's primary image.** "People just won't believe that a product is what it isn't."
- **Screaming EASY** without redefining and mechanizing the ease.
- **Announcing a price cut with no reason-why.**
- **Emotion-definers that show.** "When everybody sees your emotion words, then you've got a bad ad, and you'd better rewrite, fast."
- **Writing mechanically, device by named device.** The analysis exists to create "New Awkwardness leading to New Ability," until the techniques disappear into intuition.
- **Treating a motivation-research finding as a headline.**
