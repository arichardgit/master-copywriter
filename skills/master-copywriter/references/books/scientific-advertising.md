## Scientific Advertising — Claude C. Hopkins

**Best for:** Direct-response fundamentals — headline selection, specific proof claims, offer construction (samples, free trials, coupons), and testing copy so every dollar is traced to a customer.

**Core thesis:** Advertising is salesmanship in print, and nothing else — its only purpose is to make sales, so every question about it is settled by asking what a good salesman would do, then by traced returns rather than opinion. Because causes and effects can be measured by keyed ads and coupons, advertising has become a science governed by "laws as immutable as the law of gravitation." Anything untraced is a gamble; anything keyed can be improved until cost per customer falls by multiples.

### Laws and principles

- **Judge every ad as a salesman.** "Treat it as a salesman. Force it to justify itself. Compare it with other salesmen. Figure its cost and result. Accept no excuses which good salesmen do not make." Ads are not for general effect, not to keep your name before the people, not to aid your other salesmen.
- **Ask the two test questions on any copy decision:** "Would this help a salesman sell the goods?" "Would it help me sell them if I met the buyer in person?" This alone avoids countless mistakes.
- **Write to one typical buyer, never to the mass.** "Don't think of people in the mass. That gives you a blurred view." Picture a half-sold individual standing before you.
- **Fine writing is a disadvantage.** Literary style and clever conceits "reveal the hook. Any studied attempt to sell, if apparent, creates corresponding resistance." Be brief, clear, convincing — like a plain, sincere salesman.
- **Offer service, not a demand to buy.** People are selfish; they care nothing for your profit. "The best ads ask no one to buy." Picture the customer's side of the service until buying is the natural result. "People can be coaxed but not driven."
- **The headline's job is to select, not to attract everyone.** It is "Hey there, Bill Jones" shouted into a crowd — "create a headline which will hail those people only." A blind or clever headline draws crowds of impossible prospects and hides you from real ones.
- **Spend more time on headlines than on the body.** Hopkins spent hours on one headline, discarding scores. Identical copy under different headlines varies enormously; "it is not uncommon for a change in headlines to multiply returns from five to ten times over."
- **Run many different headlines against the same story** — twenty ads in twenty magazines — because different appeals catch different fractions of the same audience. Keep any appeal that pays even at half the pull of the best.
- **Be specific; kill every generality.** "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." Superlatives are worse than useless: they suggest exaggeration and lead readers to discount everything you say. "A man who makes a specific claim is either telling the truth or a lie" — so definite figures are accepted at full weight.
- **Tell your full story in every ad.** "When you once get a person's attention, then is the time to accomplish all you can ever hope with him." No serials, no one-claim-at-a-time. The reader is probably a once-only reader; what you leave out he may never learn.
- **Write only for new customers.** Present users won't read you. "Never waste one line of your space to say something to present users, unless you can say it in headlines."
- **The more you tell the more you sell.** Length is set by the story, not by a rule; brief ads are never keyed, and traced ads run long. Doubling well-used space doubles returns.
- **Never take direction from untraced advertising.** "Take the opinion of nobody, the verdict of nobody, who knows nothing about his returns."
- **Curiosity is one of the strongest human incentives.** Puffed Wheat was a failure until curiosity was engineered into the claim.
- **Cheapness is not an appeal; price signals value.** "Americans are extravagant. They want bargains but not cheapness." A $1,000 hat filled the floor; a $750,000 painting drew crowds only after the price was read.
- **Never make a sample free-and-cheap.** The advertiser who *bought* the package at retail for the prospect beat the one who gave his away: "An article good enough for the maker to buy is good enough for the user to buy." Giving away a 15-cent package cheapens the product — "It is like paying railroad fare after traveling on a pass."
- **Give samples only to people who have read your story and shown effort.** "First create an atmosphere of respect, a desire, an expectation." Promiscuous sampling "like waifs on the doorsteps" never pays.
- **Never charge for a sample or ask for half the price.** It kills the word "Free," retards replies, and "three in four will refuse to pay — perhaps nine in ten."
- **Make responding physically easy.** In one case mail-in inquiries cost 70 cents; the same ads bringing coupons to a local store cost 18–22 cents. On one line 70 percent of inquiries came by telephone.
- **Always include a coupon or reminder.** "A large percentage of people who read an ad and decide to act will forget that decision in five minutes."
- **Get action now.** People are dilatory; "a postponed action is too often forgotten." Put a limit on the offer, or an inducement for promptness. "Strike while the iron is hot."
- **Sell the popular appeal, not the noble one.** "People will do much to cure trouble, but people in general will do little to prevent it." Beauty beat decay-prevention on tooth paste; complexion beat eczema on soap — and mentioning eczema can destroy the beauty claim.
- **Never pay to change habits or educate a market.** Converting people to the toothbrush habit cost $20–25 a convert, and the new business is shared with rivals. Instead, "watch the development of a popular trend, the creation of new desires. Then at the right time offer to satisfy those desires."
- **Pictures must sell, not decorate.** Use a picture "only when [it forms] a better selling argument than the same amount of space set in type." An incubator ad with a decorative row of chickens raised space cost 50 percent and brought not one added sale.
- **Build a fixed individuality and never change it.** Sign ads; name the man. "A change in our characteristics would compel our best friends to get acquainted all over."
- **Choose a name that tells a story or a coined name you can own.** Cream of Wheat, Dutch Cleanser, 3-in-One Oil advertise themselves; Toasted Corn Flakes and Malted Milk built demand rivals legally shared.
- **Do your own selling; don't lean on dealers.** Loading offers, free cases and window displays usually shift trade between stores without increasing total sales.
- **Do the research.** "Genius is the art of taking pains. The advertising man who spares the midnight oil will never get very far." One scientific article in a thousand gave the caffeine campaign its keynote.

### Frameworks and formulas

**The keyed-test method (the book's master framework):**
1. Offer something that forces a traceable reply — a sample, book, free package, coupon.
2. Run competing versions varying one element: headline, picture, size, argument, offer.
3. Record cost per reply, then correct to **cost per customer and cost per dollar of sale**, because one ad brings worthless replies and another valuable ones.
4. When one method invariably proves best, it becomes a fixed principle and is applied everywhere.

**The test campaign (proving a product before national rollout):**
1. Run in four or five average towns at $3,000–$5,000 total.
2. Use a sample or free-package offer to start users quickly; learn cost per customer started.
3. Wait: do sample users buy? Do they repeat? How much? How long until profit returns selling cost?
4. "We establish averages on a small scale, and those averages always hold. … From the few thousand he learns what the millions will do."
5. Use test towns continuously to try new plans against a proven control.

**Pre-copy research routine:** read for weeks on the subject; canvass consumers house to house or by questionnaire; collect every rival's literature and claims; subscribe to clipping bureaus; route all consumer and dealer comment to the ad-writer's desk; send the product to a laboratory to convert vague claims into exact figures.

**Distribution-by-coupon formula:** publish an ad with a coupon good at any store for a full-size package; mail dealers proofs in advance. Each coupon is a cash sale at full profit, so no dealer lets those customers go elsewhere.

**The horse story (offer psychology):** "Two men came to me, each offering me a horse. … One man said, 'Try the horse for a week. If my claims are not true, come back for your money.' The other man also said, 'Try the horse for a week.' But he added, 'Come and pay me then.' I naturally bought the second man's horse." Ship first, bill later beats money-back guarantee.

**Positive-only rule:** show the face as it will appear, not the wrinkles; pretty teeth, not bad teeth; the envied, not the envious. "Tell people what to do, not what to avoid." Expect the positive ad to outpull the negative four to one.

### Swipe: verbatim lines

- "Grains puffed to 8 times the normal size."
- "Foods shot from guns."
- "125 million steam explosions caused in every kernel."
- "Multiplies itself in lather 250 times."
- "Softens the beard in one minute."
- "Maintains its creamy fullness for ten minutes on the face."
- "The final result of testing and comparing 130 formulas."
- "Our net profit is 3 percent."
- "Our prices have been reduced 25 percent."
- "Used by the peoples of 52 nations."
- "Let us help you for a week without cost or obligation."
- "I was sent here to give you a brush. I have samples here and I want you to take your choice."
- "Smoke ten, then keep them or return them, as you wish."
- "Use them a week, then do as you wish."
- "Pay in a week if you like them."
- "Try our rivals too."
- "Send now for this sample." (never: "Why do you neglect this offer?")
- "Only one sample to a home."
- "Mrs. ____, whom we know, tells us that you are one of her good customers. She has dealt with you, she says, and you do just as you agree. So we have opened with you a credit account on our books, good any time you wish."
- Proof by figures: "food value is 425 calories per pint. One pint is equal to six eggs in calories of nutriment."
- A tungsten lamp "gives three and one-third times the light" (not "more light").

### Voice and craft notes

Short declarative sentences, often four to nine words, stacked in staccato runs: "We know our cost, we know our sale, we know our profit or our loss." Paragraphs are two to five sentences. He states a law, then immediately proves it with a named case and a number. Proof is always arithmetic and always traced.

He handles objections by conceding the reader's instinct and reversing it with a salesman analogy: "Some say 'Be very brief. People will read but little.' Would you say that to a salesman?" Transitions are blunt and physical: "So in advertising," "Remember that when," "Think it over," "Bear in mind." Metaphors are homely and mechanical. He repeats key phrases verbatim across chapters rather than varying wording for style.

### What this book warns against

- Trying to sell people what they do not want, "the reason for most of the non-successes in advertising."
- Writing to please the seller, to show off, to win applause instead of sales. "Ad-writers abandon their parts. They forget they are salesmen and try to be performers."
- Slogans, clever conceits, blind headlines, eccentric illustrations, frivolity. "There are two things about which men should never joke. One is business, one is home."
- Big type and big headlines: "like loud conversation," multiplying cost without gaining worthwhile attention.
- Wasted space, borders, decorative pictures, color used merely to attract.
- Superlatives and platitudes: "Best in the world," "Lowest prices in existence," "Pure" beer.
- Serial ads and one-claim ads; brevity as a rule; boasting; crying a name plus a few generalities.
- Negative and "before and after taking" advertising; attacking rivals.
- Charging for samples, half-price offers, promiscuous sampling.
- Educating a market, arguing prevention, or campaigning on a claim that reaches one reader in a hundred.
- Fine stationery and pamphlets in follow-up: "They indicate an effort to sell on other lines than merit."
- Advertising before you have distribution.
- Above all, unkeyed spending. "Groping in the dark in this field has probably cost enough money to pay the national debt. That is what has filled the advertising graveyards."
